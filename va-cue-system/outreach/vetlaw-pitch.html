VetLaw pitch kit

How to open a conversation with a veterans' appeals firm about the effective-date and CUE tool, run the first call, and turn it into a design partnership. Built for the stage you're at: a working sample report, not a finished product.

The ask
20 minutes to learn how they work. A design partnership only if the call earns it.
What you show
The fictional Dana Reyes report, and only after they've answered your questions.
What you don't claim
Time saved, accuracy rates, or that it finds CUE. None of it is measured yet.

Why this framing, not a sales pitch

Your validation plan says outreach stays "non-pitchy" and the demo comes after the discovery questions. A firm that hears a pitch first tells you what it thinks of the pitch. A firm that walks you through its last effective-date case tells you whether the problem is real, how often it comes up, and what it costs them today. That second answer is what decides whether you build this.

So the "pitch" here is a 30-second description you give after they've talked, followed by one concrete ask.

Sequence. Send the practitioner review first (the reviewer guide is ready). Don't ask VetLaw to be that reviewer if you also want them as an interview: someone who has critiqued the report can't tell you how they work without it.

The first call: 25 minutes

Questions come from your interview guide. Take notes in their words; you'll need the exact phrases later.

  1. 0–2
    Thank them and set the frame"I'm here to learn how you do this work. If there's time at the end and you're curious, I'll show a sample."
  2. 2–15
    Their last case, in order
    • "Walk me through the last time you worked an earlier-effective-date or CUE issue."
    • "What did you open, in what order?"
    • "Roughly how many hours did it take, and who did it?"
    • "What was the most tedious or error-prone step?"
    • "When you need a regulation as it read in, say, 2008, how do you get it?"
    • "Ever find out later that you missed an earlier date? What happened?"
    • "How often do these come up, and what's a typical fee on one?"
  3. 15–16
    Ask before showing"Would it be useful to see a sample of what I'm building?" If they say no, skip to the referral question. That answer is data too.
  4. 16–21
    Three moments from the Dana Reyes report
    • Click a quote under Evidence. The scanned page opens with the words highlighted.
    • Open 38 CFR 3.155 as it read in 2008, then compare it with today's text.
    • Read the CUE card's weak point and rebuttals aloud. This is what earns trust: it argues against itself.
  5. 21–23
    The 30-second pitchOnly now. Use their words from the first section.
  6. 23–25
    The ask and the referralThe design-partner offer below, then: "Who else does a lot of this work that I should talk to?"

The 30-second version

"You said [their hardest step] takes [their hours]. What I'm building reads the claims file, finds each decision where an earlier effective date might apply, and shows it with the page it relies on and the regulation as it read on the decision date. It lists the weak points and what VA will argue back, and it never concludes there's CUE; your attorneys decide that. The goal is that every denied-claim file gets this check, not just the ones someone has time for."

If they ask what it's worth

Use the sample's own numbers and say they're estimates. Dana's earlier date is about 10 months. At today's 70% rate that is at most about $18,000 in back pay and a fee under about $3,600; the rates in 2008–09 were lower. The large cases are old denials, where the gap can run to decades. Say that such cases are rare and that this is where specialist firms make their name. Let them supply their own typical figure.

The ask: a design partnership

What VetLaw gets

  • Free use through the pilot, and founding pricing locked for a year after it.
  • Their cases shape what gets built first.
  • A second check on files they've already worked, at no cost.

What you ask for

  • A 30-minute feedback call each month for three months.
  • Two or three closed files to run once the file reader is ready, handled under the conditions below.
  • One introduction to a peer.
Before any real client file. Today the sample runs on the reviewer's own computer and nothing is uploaded. Real eFolder data is different: work out with the firm how its VA system access terms (38 C.F.R. §§ 1.600–1.603) and client consent apply before you touch a client file. Whether representatives may send eFolder data to a third-party processor is still an open question in your research. Say so if they ask.

Questions they'll ask

"Our paralegals already check effective dates."Ask how long it takes and whether every file gets it. Position the tool as the check that runs on every file, with the paralegal reviewing the result.
"AI makes things up."Every quote opens the scanned page it came from, and every regulation link opens the version in force on the decision date, so they can check it in seconds. Show the weak point and the "Needs review" list: it flags what it can't decide.
"How is this different from VA Claim Helper?"Ask what they use it for first. Then: VCH organizes the file and answers questions about it; this works out the earliest date the law supports and tests the decision for CUE using the regulation as it read then. VCH has been adding effective-date features, so don't claim they can't do it.
"Can we upload client files?"Not yet, and you'll work out the access and consent questions with them before any client file is used.
"What will it cost?"Only if asked: a flat software fee, likely $150–400 per seat a month, with per-case credits for a full workup. Never a share of their fees. Then ask how that compares with what they pay for tools now.
"Are you a lawyer or accredited?"No. It's a tool for accredited representatives, and every decision stays with them.

Don't say

  • "It finds CUE," "it wins cases," or anything that sounds like a legal conclusion.
  • Hours saved or accuracy percentages. You haven't measured them.
  • That it's ready for client files.
  • Anything about another firm's clients or another interview.
  • A price before they ask.

After the call

  • Log it in the interview tracker the same day, with their exact phrases.
  • Score it against Gate A: is this a top-three pain, do they already pay for adjacent tools, did they name "the law as it read then" unprompted, did the price band survive?
  • Send a two-line thank-you. If they agreed to a pilot, confirm the three asks in writing.
