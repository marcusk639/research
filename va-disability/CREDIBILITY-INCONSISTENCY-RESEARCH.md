## 0. The question this answers

The signed personal statement (06/17/2026) and the actual VA Form 21-0781 both assert that opioid use
began in-service (~2011, following an ankle-injury oxycodone prescription) and explicitly deny pre-service
opioid use. The clinical record contradicts this (self-reported opioid/kratom use from age 18, pre-
enlistment — see `COMPREHENSIVE-FINDINGS-2026-07.md` §0.4/§0.8). The treating clinician's forthcoming nexus
letter is expected to lead with a different, better-supported theory: substance use as self-medication
secondary to service-connected PTSD/MST. This document researches how VA adjudication and case law treat
that kind of prior-statement/clinical-record conflict, and what to do about it.

## 1. Bottom line

**Do not leave the inconsistency unaddressed, and do not write a defensive stand-alone "correction" letter
either.** File **one integrated supplemental statement** (VA Form 21-4138 or 21-10210) that:

1. Leads with the self-medication/PTSD-MST theory as the operative claim.
2. Briefly and matter-of-factly notes the earlier statement reflected incomplete recollection at the time
   it was written.
3. Lets the clinical record carry the correction rather than arguing it.

File this **before or concurrently with** the treating clinician's nexus letter, so the letter lands on an
already-consistent record rather than appearing to paper over a live contradiction.

## 2. Why

**2.1 Conflict with clinical records does not automatically sink a lay statement.**
_Buchanan v. Nicholson_, 451 F.3d 1331, 1336–37 (Fed. Cir. 2006) — verified against primary text — holds
the Board cannot deem lay evidence not credible _solely_ for lacking contemporaneous medical corroboration;
that's a factor to weigh, not dispositive. _Caluza v. Brown_, 7 Vet. App. 498, 506 (1995) (corroborated verbatim across multiple BVA
decisions at the same pincite; primary 1995 opinion not independently retrieved this session) supplies the
multi-factor credibility test the Board actually applies, which expressly includes "inconsistent
statements" and "internal inconsistency." Net effect: the existing inconsistency is real and weighable —
it won't sink the claim alone, but won't be ignored either.

**2.2 No regulation imposes a reconciliation duty.**
38 CFR § 3.102 (verified via govinfo.gov/Cornell LII) is a tie-breaker for genuinely balanced evidence —
its text states reasonable doubt "is not a means of reconciling actual conflict or a contradiction in the
evidence." Reconciliation happens through ordinary _Caluza_/_Buchanan_ weighing first, not through the
reasonable-doubt doctrine.

**2.3 No VA process or case law specifically governs proactive self-correction — this is a real gap.**
There's no dedicated "amend my prior statement" form; a correction is simply new lay evidence filed
alongside (not replacing) the earlier statement, with both retained for the rater to weigh together
(inferred from 38 U.S.C. § 7104(d)(1)'s reasons-or-bases requirement). No CAVC/BVA precedent found that
addresses voluntary correction specifically. Absent direct authority, _Caluza_'s own factors argue for
correcting: an inconsistency explained candidly reads as "internal consistency" and "facial plausibility"
working in the veteran's favor, versus one discovered cold by a rater, which reads as concealment.

**2.4 The nexus letter needs to be accurate, not to rebut the prior statement.**
_Stefl v. Nicholson_, 21 Vet. App. 120 (2007) (verified, full primary opinion) and _Reonal v. Brown_,
5 Vet. App. 458, 461 (1993) (quote corroborated across BVA decisions applying it, primary opinion not
independently pulled) hold an opinion is adequate when based on an accurate factual premise with reasoned
analysis — not when it addresses every prior lay statement. If Cherie's letter accurately recounts the
pre-service-onset/self-medication history, silence on the earlier, less-accurate statement doesn't
undermine it.

The theory itself is well-supported: _Allen v. Principi_, 237 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2001) (verified;
rehearing en banc denied, 268 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2001) — remains good law) holds 38 U.S.C. § 1110 doesn't
bar compensation for substance abuse secondary to a service-connected disability, distinct from primary
willful-misconduct abuse. 38 CFR §§ 3.301(c)(2)–(3), 3.310(a) implement this (verified, Cornell LII). M21-1,
Part X, Subpt. iv, Ch. 1, Sec. D gives a matching worked example (SC mental-health condition, secondary
substance use for symptom management) — verified against the live KnowVA page, though exact verbatim text
couldn't be captured (JS-rendered manual, worth an independent look before quoting it directly).
