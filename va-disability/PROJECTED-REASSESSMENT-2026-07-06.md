## 2. What each addition actually does, mapped to 38 CFR 4.130

_(4.130 text verified verbatim in `100-PERCENT-SCHEDULAR-PATHWAY.md` §1, sourced to Cornell LII/eCFR —
not re-verified here since it's already primary-sourced and current per that document's Status: complete.)_

**OCD (concrete rituals/checking/intrusive thoughts).** "Obsessional rituals which interfere with
routine activities" is a **70%-tier symptom**, verbatim. Right now the diagnosis is real and now properly
flagged in prep materials, but the actual described _behavior_ — what checking, counting, or intrusive-
thought pattern looks like day to day — exists nowhere in the written record. An independent 8-year
cohabiting witness describing concrete instances is a direct, textual anchor at 70%, and it's evidence a
rater/examiner can point to that isn't just the veteran's own report.

**Despair / passive suicidal ideation — now specific, and this is a genuinely strong addition.**
_Bankhead v. Shulkin_, 29 Vet. App. 10 (2017) (already verified in-corpus) holds passive ideation — a wish
not to wake up, a belief others would be better off — is itself a real symptom, textually named at the
**70%-tier** ("suicidal ideation," no qualifier on passive vs. active). You've described Erica's planned
content as: he's said he'd be better off dead, that she'd be better off if he were dead, and that this is
present daily upon waking. This is about as squarely on-point as _Bankhead_-type evidence gets — it names
both prongs the case addresses (a wish not to exist, a belief others would be better off) and gives a
frequency (daily). Two consequences: (a) it directly corroborates the personal statement's contested "SI
hits every day" claim from a fully independent source, which meaningfully changes the SI-reconciliation
framing — this is no longer "the veteran's own report vs. a chart note," it's "two independent accounts of
daily SI vs. one chart note 8 days apart," a real shift in the weight of evidence, not just more of it;
and (b) it's an explicit, named symptom a rater/examiner doesn't have to infer from softer language. The
only caveat: this still needs to be true and hers, described because it happened, not because it's the
strongest possible phrasing — and it should be prepared for consistently with whatever the C&P exam
discussion of SI frequency ends up being, so it doesn't read as sharper than everything else in the file.

**Cognitive symptoms (memory/concentration).** These sit closer to general severity support than a single
sharp textual anchor — "memory loss for names of close relatives, own occupation, or own name" is the
specific 100%-tier language, and generic memory lapses/concentration trouble described by a spouse won't
reach that unless genuinely that severe. Treat this as supporting evidence for overall severity, not a
100%-tier anchor on its own, unless what she actually recalls is that specific and that severe.

**Hygiene/ADL neglect — now the single most consequential item in this whole reassessment.** The
regulation draws a real distinction here that's worth stating exactly:

- **70%-tier:** "neglect of personal appearance and hygiene" — a description of a state/behavior.
- **100%-tier:** "intermittent inability to perform activities of daily living (including maintenance of
  minimal personal hygiene)" — a functional-incapacity framing, but note "intermittent" — it does **not**
  require constant incapacity, only that it recurs.

You've described Erica's planned content as "she has to help me take care of myself" — that's not a
description of neglect, it's a description of needing another person to perform the task, which is
squarely the **100%-tier "intermittent inability to perform ADLs (including hygiene)"** language, not the
70%-tier "neglect" language. This is genuinely significant: per `CLAIM-ASSESSMENT.md` Part 2, no record
anywhere in this ~600-page corpus uses any 100%-tier symptom language at all — this would be the first.
The value of this piece of evidence is unusually high for that reason alone. The only thing left to get
right is frequency/pattern — "intermittent" is satisfied by recurring bad stretches, it doesn't need to be
constant, so let her describe how often and for how long this actually happens, in her own words, rather
than assuming "daily" or "constant" is required.

**December 2024 elaboration — now causally tied, not just chronological.** You've described this as
Erica attributing the job loss specifically to his depression and SUD, not just placing it in time. That's
a stronger contribution than a bare date confirmation: it closes the job-loss-year gap (CLAIM-ASSESSMENT.md
item 7) and the "gainfully employed" (Aug 2024) vs. later-collapse sequencing gap
(`COMPREHENSIVE-FINDINGS-2026-07.md` §9 item 7), and — because it's causal, not just sequential — it
directly supports the "total occupational impairment" prong as _caused by_ the service-connected
psychiatric conditions specifically, which is what the schedular argument actually needs (not just "he lost
a job," but "the psychiatric conditions caused him to lose it"). A spouse's own independent, contemporaneous
account of this is exactly the genuine, undirected evidence the reassess methodology's guardrail calls for
before any higher-rating argument can rest on the deteriorated-employment picture (see
`COMPREHENSIVE-FINDINGS-2026-07.md` §8) — it predates any strategic decision about how to frame the claim.
