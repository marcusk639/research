## Draft

My name is Erica, and I've been with my husband Marcus since 2018. We met in a 12-step recovery program — I'm in long-term recovery myself, with 21 years sober, so when I write about what addiction looks like in someone I love, I'm not writing as an outside observer. I know what it takes to recover, and I've watched him try and fail to get there more than once, not from a lack of trying, but because of what he's carrying underneath it.

The addiction was never really the problem. It was the symptom. Underneath it is something we've never fully been able to talk about directly: he told me, early in our relationship, that he was sexually assaulted during his military service. It's not something we've processed together in the way I think we probably should have. But I've lived next to what it does to him for eight years, and I want to describe that as plainly as I can.

He carries a level of anxiety that's hard to put into words. He worries obsessively, over things large and small, and he can't turn it off. Alongside the worry is a need to control every possible outcome, which sends him into ruminating on worst-case scenarios in almost every situation. To me, he's always looked like someone trying to manage a danger that isn't actually in the room. He's jumpy, easily startled by ordinary things, and becomes irritable when it happens. I've come to understand that as hypervigilance — a trauma response, not a personality trait.

His sleep has never been normal in the time I've known him. His insomnia traces back to his military service, when he was first prescribed Ambien, and it never really resolved. I've been woken up by him yelling in his sleep during nightmares, and he's had panic attacks in the middle of the night. Over the years he's been prescribed a rotating cast of heavy sedatives trying to manage it — Gabapentin, Seroquel, Quviviq among them. I've seen him sleepwalk. I've seen him drive in that condition. I've watched him put himself, me, and our dog at risk, driving impaired by exhaustion or medication in ways that terrified me.

I've seen him in genuine despair. There have been many mornings he woke up in tears because he didn't want to face another day. Watching someone I love reach that point, and not knowing what I'd find when I got home some days, is a fear I can't fully describe.

The trauma from the assault has reached into the most intimate parts of our life together. Our physical relationship became difficult, and intimacy itself got tangled up with what happened to him — it's not something either of us finds easy to write about, but it belongs in this story, because it's not just in his past. It's in our present.

His depression and anxiety have also left him extremely isolated. He stopped seeing friends, avoided people, and for years there was no social life at all. He becomes withdrawn and disconnected, even from me.

I've always believed his extreme workaholism was a trauma response, not ambition — a way of escaping what was underneath, that never actually worked and just postponed the next collapse. In 2018, early in our relationship, I watched him nearly lose his job because he was falling apart and couldn't function. Getting into treatment that year is what saved it. He lost his job again in 2023, and it took him about six months to find work again — we nearly lost our home over it. In 2026 it happened once more: he went into residential treatment in the spring, and by June he'd lost his job again. After that, we made the decision together that he needed to leave his corporate tech career entirely, and we moved back to his hometown so he could focus on getting well instead of on a career that was actively making him worse.

The relapse cycle I've witnessed started in 2018, right at the beginning of our relationship. He already had a history of addiction before we met, but what I saw was a pattern: the intensity building, the anxiety, the obsessing, the hopelessness, until it became too much, and then the relapse. Detox, rehab, over and over, without being able to hold onto recovery for long. He's tried therapy, medication, 12-step groups, meditation, every lifestyle change either of us could think of. I've watched him reach for help again and again and get pulled back under by the same pain he can't seem to get out from beneath. For him, the depression, the anxiety, the obsessive thinking, and the need for control aren't separate problems — they're symptoms of the same underlying wound, and the addiction is how he's tried to escape it.

The financial toll has been real — a significant amount of money over the years spent on substances, secretly, and the stress of that secrecy changed things between us for a while, in ways that hurt. We've been in couples counseling on and off since 2020. There was a point where I seriously considered whether I could stay. We spent time apart. What kept me was not wanting to give up on him, on the hope that recovery was possible, and the hope that our marriage could be repaired if he did the work. I wanted to fight this together, not against each other.

I'm writing this because I've lived beside it for eight years. What happened to him in the service didn't stay there. It followed him into his sleep, his body, his work, our finances, our bed, and our marriage. I don't think most people understand how serious, how constant, and how persistent that kind of thing can be, once you've actually lived next to it.
