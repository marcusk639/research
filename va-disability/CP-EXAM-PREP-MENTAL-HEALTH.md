# C&P Exam Prep — Mental Health

> **Status:** active · **Created:** 2026-07-05 · **Supersedes:** `ClaudeDisabilityAssets/CP-Exam-Prep-Mental-Health-Marcus-Klein.pdf`, which is missing OCD content and predates the current framing strategy.
> **Basis:** `CLAIM-ASSESSMENT.md` and `100-PERCENT-SCHEDULAR-PATHWAY.md` in this same directory.
> **Purpose:** prepare for the C&P (Compensation & Pension) exam — the single most outcome-determinative event in this claim. The examiner's selection on the mental disorders DBQ ("deficiencies in most areas" vs. "total occupational and social impairment") is what the rating will most directly follow.

## The one thing that matters most

Describe your **worst and typical** days, not your best days. People tend to present their most composed self in a formal evaluation — that instinct actively works against you here. If the examiner sees you more put-together than you actually are day-to-day, the rating will reflect that, not your real baseline. Composure during the exam is not evidence of functioning; describe what a bad day and an average day actually look like, concretely.

## The framing goal

VA's 100% tier requires **total occupational AND social impairment** — both, not just one. Don't frame everything around "I can't work." Describe both:

- **Occupational:** concentration, decision-making, reliability, ability to sustain a work routine, what happened at your last several jobs and why.
- **Social:** relationships (with Erica, family, friends), isolation, ability to initiate or sustain connections, what your daily social world actually looks like now.

A claim that only makes the occupational case will map more naturally to a lower tier or to TDIU-style reasoning, not schedular 100%, regardless of how severe the occupational impairment is.

## Symptom checklist — describe honestly, don't hide, don't overstate

- **Suicidal ideation:** be honest and be _specific and consistent_. If it's present, describe frequency (daily/weekly), whether it's passive (thoughts without plan/intent) or active, and how long it's been part of your experience. **Important:** a recent clinical note (6/25/26) recorded "denies SI" while your personal statement (6/17/26) describes it as daily — if this comes up, be ready to explain the actual pattern honestly (fluctuation is real and normal) rather than being caught by surprise at the discrepancy.
- **Depression:** frequency, duration, what a depressive episode actually looks like day-to-day, anhedonia, sleep/appetite/energy changes.
- **Anxiety/panic:** frequency of panic attacks, physical symptoms (racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath), what triggers them, how long they last, whether they occur in specific settings (work, crowds) or unpredictably.
- **Obsessions/compulsions (OCD) — don't leave this out.** If genuinely present: describe intrusive thoughts, any rituals or checking behaviors, how much time they consume, and how they interfere with routine activities or work. VA's own rating criteria specifically list "obsessional rituals which interfere with routine activities" as a named symptom — this is directly relevant if it's real for you.
- **Hypervigilance/avoidance:** startle response, scanning environments, avoiding reminders of the stressor, avoiding certain people/situations/topics.
- **Intrusion/flashbacks:** nightmares, intrusive memories, dissociation.
- **Sleep:** current sleep pattern, medications, how long-standing the disruption is (note the in-service Ambien/Restoril treatment as historical continuity if relevant).
- **Substance use:** be honest about current status. Frame it, if genuinely true, as connected to managing the psychiatric symptoms rather than a separate, unrelated problem — this is the theory the actual treatment record supports.
- **Irritability/impulse control:** unprovoked irritability, any history of anger outbursts.
- **Concentration/memory:** difficulty focusing, forgetfulness, difficulty completing tasks.

## Be ready for these specific questions

- **"Why wasn't this reported at the time / why did you deny trauma history in [year] treatment records?"** Have an honest, prepared answer — not a defensive one. The specific instances an examiner could point to: a **2015 VA intake (Jennings CBOC)** with an explicit clinician-recorded denial ("Patient denies experiencing military sexual trauma (MST)") and a negative 4-question PC-PTSD screen; **two separate 2018 Victory Addiction Recovery Center intakes** (8/25/18 and 10/1/18) with explicit denials of any history of abuse/trauma/exploitation. Don't be caught naming these cold — you already know the dates and facilities before the exam does. Delayed and non-linear disclosure is well-documented in trauma literature generally and MST literature specifically; many people don't disclose to unrelated intake staff (addiction treatment, general medicine) even while later disclosing in trauma-focused care. If asked, explain the actual sequence honestly: disclosed to fellow service members during service, but didn't disclose to medical providers consistently for years afterward — that's a common, explainable pattern, not evidence the event didn't happen.
- **"Your records show anxiety/depression and substance use going back to around age 18, before you enlisted — how does that fit a service-connection claim?"** Answer honestly: yes, that self-report is in the record. It doesn't rule out service connection — it points toward the conditions being _aggravated beyond their natural progression_ by service and post-service events, or the SUD being connected _secondary to_ the in-service PTSD/psychiatric conditions as self-medication, rather than a claim that everything started fresh in service. Say this plainly if asked; don't downplay or contradict your own prior self-reports.
- **"There's a diagnosis of Amphetamine-type Substance Use Disorder in an April-May 2026 record — what is that?"** Be honest that this diagnosis (F15.20, Harmony Junction Recovery) doesn't have much explanation in your own understanding either — don't guess at or overstate a history you don't recall if you're not sure what it refers to; it's fine to say you're not certain what drove that specific coding and that you'd want to ask the treating facility directly, rather than inventing a narrative to fill the gap.
- **"You've been employed at a high level for years — how does that square with total impairment?"** Be honest: describe the actual pattern — sustaining work through significant effort and cost (the "work until I burn out, then relapse" cycle documented in your own treatment records), not evidence of being unaffected. Be specific about the sequencing if asked: a 2024 Starlite discharge record notes you were "gainfully employed" at that specific time (accurate then) — describe honestly how your employment situation has changed since, through 2025–2026, rather than letting the exam treat the 2024 snapshot as still current.
- **"What does a typical day look like right now?"** This is your chance to describe the real, current picture — don't minimize it and don't overstate it.

## What NOT to do

- Don't claim symptoms you don't have to hit a specific rating tier (e.g., don't claim ADL impairment, disorientation, or memory loss for names/occupation if that's not genuinely your experience — the existing record wisely doesn't overreach here, and that consistency is a credibility strength).
- Don't minimize out of pride or habit. The instinct to appear composed and capable is itself something to name to the examiner if relevant ("I'm used to holding it together for exams/interviews, but that's not what my average day looks like").
- Don't guess at legal standards or cite regulations to the examiner — describe your actual experience and let the clinical picture speak for itself.
