The rule that governs every page

This document never tells you what symptoms to have. It tells you what the examiner is measuring and reminds you to give the whole, accurate truth at its real depth — worst days and typical days, with frequency, duration and consequences. Exaggeration is fraud (38 U.S.C. § 6103; 18 U.S.C. § 1001) and is also the fastest way to lose a true claim: validity testing and record comparison exist to catch it. Minimizing is the opposite failure, and it is the one most veterans actually commit.

Research and preparation assistance only — not legal or medical advice. Review with your VSO / VA-accredited representative. Contains personal health information; do not send to VA (internal prep material is never evidence).

Two health and safety notes, before anything about the claim

You told us you are currently using opioids and looking for treatment. Combined with daily suicidal thoughts, that is a genuine overdose risk. Keep naloxone (Narcan — available without a prescription at pharmacies) where Erica can reach it. If thoughts turn active, call or text 988 and press 1 (Veterans Crisis Line), or text 838255. The VA can start medication treatment for opioid use disorder quickly — ask the Austin VA (or call 877-222-8387, the VA general health line) for same-day/urgent mental-health or SUD access. Getting into treatment does not hurt your claim; it helps you and it documents your condition.

Do not attend the exam impaired. An examiner who observes intoxication will document it, may stop the exam, and may attribute everything they see to the substance. Do not stop opioids abruptly on your own to "look better" either — unmanaged withdrawal is dangerous and will also distort the exam. If you are in withdrawal or have used recently, the honest move is to say so at the start. Talk to a prescriber about safe management before Wednesday if you can.

Part A — How this exam actually works

Knowing what the examiner is filling in is the single best antidote to a thin exam.

A1. Who you'll see and what they produce

  • Contract examiner, not your clinician. VES (Veterans Evaluation Services, a Maximus company) is a VA contractor. Your examiner will be a licensed psychologist or psychiatrist — sometimes a psychiatric nurse practitioner or LCSW for some non-initial exams, but an initial PTSD exam requires a psychologist or psychiatrist. They have no treatment relationship with you, and VES's own FAQ describes the exam as the same exam a VA facility provider performs.
  • The form. Because PTSD is not yet service-connected, expect the Initial PTSD Disability Benefits Questionnaire (VA Form 21-0960P-4 structure), which also captures your other mental diagnoses. Its companion, the Mental Disorders DBQ (21-0960P-2), has the same core items. Either way the examiner fills the same skeleton: diagnoses → differentiation between diagnoses → one overall impairment checkbox → history (pre-, during and post-military; social, occupational, mental-health, legal, substance) → stressor(s) and Criterion A → PTSD criteria B–H → symptom checklist → behavioral observations → other symptoms → competency to manage funds → remarks/testing → medical opinion(s).
  • Time. Plan for roughly 60–90 minutes; initial PTSD exams with records review and testing can run longer, and some examiners split testing into a separate session. A 20–30 minute exam on a claim this complex is a red flag (see Part E).
  • Records. The examiner usually reviews your claims file (VBMS) beforehand: your 21-0781 and personal statement, the Reyes and Poquette buddy statements, Erica's statement, Cherie Mills's letters, your VA records (including the 2015 Jennings CBOC visit), and whatever private treatment records were submitted. Assume they have read the worst page in your file.

A3. What the rater does with the exam

The examiner does not assign the percentage — a rater (RVSR) does. The rater must evaluate "all the evidence of record that bears on occupational and social impairment rather than solely on the examiner's assessment … at the moment of the examination" (38 CFR 4.126(a)). Cherie's signed letters, Erica's statement, the DASS-21/WHODAS, and the treatment records are all weighed. In practice, though, the examiner's single impairment checkbox carries the most weight. A thin exam is fixable afterward, but it costs months. The goal Wednesday is an exam that captures what is already in your file and what is true today.

Two rules shape the 70-versus-100 decision:

  • The symptom lists are examples, not checklists (Mauerhan v. Principi). What counts is symptoms of similar severity, frequency and duration that actually cause the level of impairment (Vazquez-Claudio v. Shinseki). Frequency, duration and consequence are what the examiner needs from you, not labels.
  • 100% requires total occupational AND total social impairment, and VA may not rate solely on social impairment (4.126(b)). Since you are unemployed, the occupational side is the stronger prong. The social prong — with a marriage, AA, and a weekly outing on the record — is where 100% is won or lost.

A6. Telehealth with VES — practical rules

  • Room: private, quiet, door closed, good light on your face, phone/laptop on a stable surface at eye level, charger plugged in. Have the VES phone number (877-637-8387) and your appointment confirmation handy in case the video drops. Log in 10–15 minutes early — VA warns late arrival can cancel an exam.
  • ID: expect to show photo ID on camera and confirm your identity and location (they need to know where you are for emergencies).
  • Erica: family members may generally sit in only with the examiner's approval and may not take part unless invited. At the start, say: "My wife is here; she helps me with daily things and with remembering. Is it okay if she stays, and could she add anything at the end if you want collateral information?" If the examiner asks her to step out, especially for the assault questions, agree without argument — refusing can end the exam. Ask whether she can speak with them for a few minutes afterward.
  • Recording: do not record. Contractor telehealth exams are generally not recorded, and recording without permission can end the exam. Erica can write notes after the call.
  • Notes: VA says you don't need to bring anything. A short sheet of dates, medication names and your frequency numbers is reasonable — glance at it, don't read answers from it. If asked, say plainly: "I wrote down dates so I'd get them right." Scripted-sounding answers hurt credibility.
  • What the camera sees is the mental-status exam. The examiner will record appearance, grooming, eye contact, speech, affect and behavior. If Erica helped you shower, shave or dress for the call, say so — otherwise "well-groomed" goes on the DBQ and quietly undercuts your strongest 100%-tier fact.

A7. The examiner's mindset — and the traps that come with it

  • They test consistency, not just severity. Every answer is compared against your filed statements and records. The biggest risk is not a bad symptom; it's an answer that contradicts something you signed without an explanation.
  • Open questions, then pointed ones. "Tell me about your mood" comes first, then "How many days in the last two weeks…". Give the concrete number when you get the pointed version.
  • "How are you doing?" is not small talk. "Fine" or "good" can be charted as self-reported mood. Answer it truthfully.
  • Telescoping. Examiners write in present tense ("He reports panic attacks a few times a month") from whatever you said last. If something has changed, say when.
  • Best-day bias. The exam is a planned appointment, often on a better day, with Erica's help. Describe the typical week and the worst stretches as well as today.
  • Validity testing (Part C) catches both over-endorsement and inconsistent responding. Read every item; answer each honestly.

A8. Competency to manage VA funds — know the consequence before you answer

The DBQ asks whether you can manage your own financial affairs. If the honest answer is that Erica manages money because of your substance use (her statement references roughly $30,000 spent on drugs), say so. That is real evidence of severity. Know what it can lead to: VA may propose an incompetency rating (38 CFR 3.353) — it needs a definite medical statement, gives you notice and a chance to respond, and resolves reasonable doubt in favor of competency — and, if finalized, VA's fiduciary program (38 CFR Part 13) appoints someone (often a spouse) to receive payments. That is not a punishment and it does not reduce the rating. But it should not be a surprise, and you should never shade the truth either way to avoid it.
