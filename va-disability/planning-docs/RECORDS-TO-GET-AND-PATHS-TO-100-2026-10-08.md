## How to read this

**Part 1** lists the records that could genuinely strengthen the claim. They are ranked by what each one proves and which question it answers:

- **(A) Service connection.** Did the assault happen in service, and is PTSD tied to it?
- **(B) Rating level.** How severe is the impairment?
- **(T) TDIU.** Can you hold a job?

**Part 2** lays out every route to a 100% rating that doesn't depend on TDIU, with an honest assessment of each.

One rule applies throughout. A record helps only if it shows what actually happened. Get records to fill gaps, not to build a narrative. If a record turns out to cut against the claim, it's still part of the truth, and VA may obtain it anyway.

Even with every plausible secondary condition, a 70% mental-health rating combines to about **90%, not 100%**. Each added condition counts against the "remaining" percentage, so its value shrinks. One example: hypertension at 10% would add under 1 point.

Secondary conditions are still worth pursuing. Sleep apnea at 50% takes a 70% rating to **90%**, which pays meaningfully more. ED doesn't change the percentage, but it can qualify for **SMC(k)**, a separate monthly payment added on top of your rating (38 CFR 3.350(a)(1)).

### Route 3 — temporary 100% for stays over 21 days

**What the regulation says** (38 CFR 4.29, verified):

- A 100% rating applies when a service-connected disability "required hospital treatment in a Department of Veterans Affairs or an **approved hospital** for a period in excess of 21 days."
- It runs from "the first day of continuous hospitalization" to the last day of the month of discharge.

**What's unsettled:**

- **"Approved hospital" isn't defined** in the regulation.
- VA's adjudication manual (M21-1 VIII.iv.8.B, read via a third-party copy and **not yet verified in KnowVA**) suggests three things:
  - non-VA stays count when approved by VA on or before admission;
  - residential or domiciliary residence alone doesn't qualify;
  - what matters is whether the treatment was hospital-level.
- Board decisions conflict on residential stays.

**Your potential case:** Harmony Junction, 4/17–5/11/2026, was about 24–25 days of detox plus residential treatment, with psychiatric visits. That's over 21 days. But it was private, not VA-authorized, and largely residential. If PTSD is service connected, with SUD secondary, the questions are:

1. Does the effective date of any grant reach back to April 2026?
2. Was Harmony's detox phase "hospital-level" treatment?
3. Would VA treat it as approved?

**Honest assessment:** worth raising with James, because the payoff is a month or two at the 100% rate. It's uncertain, though, and it's never a permanent 100%. Future stays over 21 days at a **VA** facility, or one VA approves in advance, would qualify far more clearly.

**Don't** schedule or extend treatment to reach 21 days. Treatment length should be clinical. Planning a stay around a rating is the kind of thing that damages a claim.

### What would have to be true for a genuine 100% (Route 1)

For reference, so you can recognize it if it ever becomes your reality, and not as a target to hit. A 100% mental-health rating fits when the records consistently show:

- inability to work in any setting;
- **and** social impairment approaching total: no meaningful relationships functioning, not just strained ones;
- often with symptoms like inability to maintain minimal hygiene without someone else doing it, gross impairment in thinking or communication, persistent delusions or hallucinations, or persistent danger of hurting yourself or others.

Your current honest picture is 70%. If that changes, your treating providers' records will show it on their own.
