6. **Overall impairment:** one of six boxes. These map to 0 / 10 / 30 / 50 / 70 / 100%. There's no 90% level.
7. **Symptom checklist:** the VA rating-schedule symptoms, including suicidal ideation, neglect of hygiene, obsessional rituals, and persistent danger to self.
8. **Behavioral observations:** how you present on camera.
9. **Competency:** "Is the Veteran capable of managing his or her financial affairs?" See the note in section 5.
10. **Remarks:** including any test results.

The form also tells the examiner to stop the interview and get help if a mental-health emergency happens during it.

**The MST marker opinion.** VA rules let behavior changes after an assault (markers) back up an in-service assault that was never reported. Examples include substance abuse, depression or panic with no clear cause, changes in work performance, disregard for authority, and changes in relationships (38 CFR 3.304(f)(5); M21-1 VIII.iv.1.E.1.d via [RateMyVSO](https://ratemyvso.net/dc/m21/part-viii-sub-iv-ch-1-sec-e)).

- **Post-service changes can count** if they can reasonably be associated with the event.
- **Her opinion carries weight.** VA treats it as "significant probative evidence" (M21-1 VIII.iv.1.E.1.d), and it has to be "credible, unequivocal, and nonspeculative" (E.1.e).
- **Not reporting it in service can't be held against you.** Per _AZ v. Shinseki_, an examiner can't use the absence of an in-service report against you, and an opinion that does "should be returned as insufficient" (M21-1 VIII.iv.1.C.2.a via [Navigating VA](https://navigatingva.com/law-policy/va-policy/articles/554400000177713/)).

## 2A. How the interview usually runs

VA's examiner training for Initial PTSD exams says the examiner reviews your claims file **before** the exam, orients you, and then gathers history and symptoms. The DBQ is for writing up the report, "not meant as a guide for the examination itself" ([VES examiner course, DMA Initial PTSD Examination](https://vesservices.com/veswp/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/DMA_Initial_Post_Traumatic_Stress_Disorder_PTSD_Examination-.pdf)). A typical sequence, with the time budget VA's older best-practice manual suggested ([Best Practice Manual for PTSD C&P Exams](https://abcdocz.com/doc/297038/best-practice-manual-for-posttraumatic-stress-disorder--p...), secondary copy):

1. **Orientation and consent.** She'll explain that she's an independent evaluator, not your therapist; the report goes to VA; confidentiality has limits; honesty matters and exaggeration hurts a claim; and the exam can be upsetting. Examiner guides recommend all of these ([Worthen & Moering](https://scispace.com/pdf/a-practical-guide-to-conducting-va-compensation-and-pension-22w721kiy4.pdf)). She can't tell you results or answer questions about the claims process ([VA.gov](https://www.va.gov/resources/va-claim-exam/)).
2. **Military history and the trauma** (~20 min). Your service, then the stressor.
3. **PTSD symptoms** (~40 min). Often in CAPS-5 style: for each symptom, **how often in the past month, how intense, since when, and how it affects you.**
4. **Mental status exam.** Mostly observation (see below).
5. **Psychosocial history and functioning.** Before, during and after service: family, marriage, work, education, legal, substance use, medications, treatment.
6. **Risk and closing.** Suicide/self-harm questions, possibly competency, then "anything else?"

The order varies by examiner. Some cover everything in 45 minutes; some take two hours or more.

**The mental status exam (MSE)** covers: appearance and behavior; speech; thought process and content (including obsessions, delusions, hallucinations); mood (what you report); affect (what she observes); and cognition (orientation, memory, concentration) (VES examiner course, above). On video she'll notice grooming, eye contact, restlessness, how you speak, and whether your emotion fits what you're describing.

- **Come as you normally are.** Don't clean up specially, and don't dress down to look worse. Either one makes the observation inaccurate.
- If you're having a better-than-usual day, say so and describe a typical one.

**MST detail.** The examiner has to document the stressor enough to assess it, and examiners are often asked to judge whether the evidence is consistent with MST. A 2026 National Academies report notes this often leads examiners to ask veterans to retell the event, and calls for a trauma-informed approach ([NASEM, _Improving Disability Evaluations for Veterans Who Have Experienced MST_](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/29453/chapter/7)).

- Your 21-0781 and personal statement are in the file. If it's very hard to go into detail, you can say so and ask whether what's in your statement is enough. Then answer what she still needs.
- You can ask for a short break.
- Memory gaps from incapacitation are expected. Say what you remember and what you don't.

**How to describe symptoms so they're usable.** Rating language turns on **frequency, severity and duration** (_Vazquez-Claudio v. Shinseki_, 713 F.3d 112 (Fed. Cir. 2013), as cited in `CLAIM-RECONCILIATION-WORKLIST.md` E1; not re-verified in this pass). For each symptom, have:

- **How often** in the past month ("4–5 nights a week," not "all the time" unless it literally is);
- **How bad** at its worst and on a typical day;
- **How long** an episode lasts and how long it's been this way;
- **One recent concrete example** with what it cost you (a missed appointment, a day in bed, leaving a meeting).

Estimates are fine; say "about" or "roughly." If you don't know, say so. Don't invent a number.

## 6. Testing, and why careful answers protect you

- **What she may use:** checklists such as the PCL-5 (20 items, 5–10 minutes, not a stand-alone diagnosis), a structured interview such as the CAPS-5 (45–60 minutes), and possibly a longer inventory with **validity scales** (MMPI-2/2-RF/3 or PAI) or a short screener such as the M-FAST or SIMS ([VA/DoD PTSD assessment guidance](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/MH/ptsd/PTSD-Assessment_508.pdf); [National Center for PTSD, PCL-5](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/assessment/documents/using-PCL5.pdf); Worthen & Moering, [practical guide for C&P examiners](https://scispace.com/pdf/a-practical-guide-to-conducting-va-compensation-and-pension-22w721kiy4.pdf)).
- **Many exams use only an interview.** VA research found structured instruments are rarely used in C&P exams ([VA HSR&D Forum, 2012](https://hsrd.research.va.gov/publications/forum/may12/may12-6.cfm)). Don't be surprised either way.
- **What validity scales do:** they flag answers that endorse unusually many, or unusual, symptoms. Examiners are taught to separate a "cry for help" pattern from extreme distress versus actual exaggeration. They report results in Remarks, and a clearly invalid profile can lead an examiner to defer or withhold a diagnosis (Worthen & Moering). The National Academies note these scales can't, on their own, prove malingering ([NAP, 2007](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/nap-cgi/skimchap.cgi?chap=85%E2%80%93112&recid=11870)). In practice, though, an invalid result undercuts the whole exam.
- **What this means for you:** read each item, answer for the time period it asks about, and pick the answer that's true, not the strongest one. Rate symptoms you don't have as absent. Accurate answers are what make your real symptoms count.
