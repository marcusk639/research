> **Purpose:** prepare for the C&P (Compensation & Pension) exam — the single most outcome-determinative event in this claim. The examiner's selection on the mental disorders DBQ ("deficiencies in most areas" vs. "total occupational and social impairment") is what the rating will most directly follow.

## The one thing that matters most

Describe your **worst and typical** days, not your best days. People tend to present their most composed self in a formal evaluation — that instinct actively works against you here. If the examiner sees you more put-together than you actually are day-to-day, the rating will reflect that, not your real baseline. Composure during the exam is not evidence of functioning; describe what a bad day and an average day actually look like, concretely.

## The framing goal

VA's 100% tier requires **total occupational AND social impairment** — both, not just one. Don't frame everything around "I can't work." Describe both:

- **Occupational:** concentration, decision-making, reliability, ability to sustain a work routine, what happened at your last several jobs and why.
- **Social:** relationships (with Erica, family, friends), isolation, ability to initiate or sustain connections, what your daily social world actually looks like now.

A claim that only makes the occupational case will map more naturally to a lower tier or to TDIU-style reasoning, not schedular 100%, regardless of how severe the occupational impairment is.

## Symptom checklist — describe honestly, don't hide, don't overstate

- **Suicidal ideation:** be honest and be _specific and consistent_. If it's present, describe frequency (daily/weekly), whether it's passive (thoughts without plan/intent) or active, and how long it's been part of your experience. **Important:** a recent clinical note (6/25/26) recorded "denies SI" while your personal statement (6/17/26) describes it as daily — if this comes up, be ready to explain the actual pattern honestly (fluctuation is real and normal) rather than being caught by surprise at the discrepancy.
- **Depression:** frequency, duration, what a depressive episode actually looks like day-to-day, anhedonia, sleep/appetite/energy changes.
- **Anxiety/panic:** frequency of panic attacks, physical symptoms (racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath), what triggers them, how long they last, whether they occur in specific settings (work, crowds) or unpredictably.
- **Obsessions/compulsions (OCD) — don't leave this out.** If genuinely present: describe intrusive thoughts, any rituals or checking behaviors, how much time they consume, and how they interfere with routine activities or work. VA's own rating criteria specifically list "obsessional rituals which interfere with routine activities" as a named symptom — this is directly relevant if it's real for you.
- **Hypervigilance/avoidance:** startle response, scanning environments, avoiding reminders of the stressor, avoiding certain people/situations/topics. This includes persistently monitoring your surroundings to check that no one is present who intends harm to you or your family — a well-established, core PTSD symptom, straightforward to describe honestly.
- **Intrusion/flashbacks:** nightmares, intrusive memories, dissociation.
- **Sleep:** current sleep pattern, medications, how long-standing the disruption is (the in-service Ambien/Restoril treatment is **not yet verified** — no STR or pharmacy record in hand — so describe it from memory, not as documented). Post-service insomnia treatment is documented: "H/O Insomnia" as a diagnostic impression and trazodone for insomnia at Acadiana in 2016, and doxepin for insomnia at discharge in 2017 (Acadiana PDF pp. 7, 10, 21).
- **Substance use:** be honest about current status. Frame it, if genuinely true, as connected to managing the psychiatric symptoms rather than a separate, unrelated problem — this is the theory the actual treatment record supports.
- **Irritability/impulse control:** unprovoked irritability, any history of anger outbursts. You've indicated you expect to describe getting irritable and even angry — describe this honestly, including frequency and what triggers it. Note: VA's regulatory example is "unprovoked irritability with periods of violence," but per _Mauerhan v. Principi_ the listed symptoms are illustrative, not a checklist — real, frequent irritability/anger counts even without any violence, so don't feel you need to overstate this into something it isn't.
- **Concentration/memory:** difficulty focusing, forgetfulness, difficulty completing tasks. You've indicated you rely on Erica to help you remember certain things — describe this concretely and honestly (what kinds of things, how often). Be precise about severity: general forgetfulness/reliance on a spouse supports the overall severity picture and the "deficiencies in most areas" (70%-tier) language; the regulation's specific 100%-tier language ("memory loss for names of close relatives, own occupation, or own name") describes something more severe and specific — only describe it that way if it's genuinely that severe. Don't round up.
- **Negative, catastrophic beliefs about the world (a real PTSD symptom — describe it accurately, not as "delusions"):** you've indicated a persistent, exaggerated conviction that the world is on the brink of destruction, headed toward civil war or dictatorship. This maps to a well-documented PTSD symptom category — DSM-5 Criterion D's "persistent and exaggerated negative beliefs or expectations about oneself, others, or the world" (its own example: "the world is completely dangerous"). **Important distinction, confirmed with you directly:** you describe this as anxious, catastrophic preoccupation that you can recognize as probably excessive when you step back — not a fixed, unshakeable certainty. That's the accurate way to describe it to Cherie and the examiner. Avoid the word "delusional" — clinically, a delusion is a fixed false belief resistant to contrary evidence, which is a different (and much rarer) thing than catastrophic/anxious preoccupation, and using that specific word inaccurately risks inviting scrutiny for something else entirely rather than accurately conveying a real, already-recognized PTSD symptom.

## If a specific inconsistency is raised (SI frequency, disclosure years, etc.)

Per the veteran (2026-07-06), the general approach for anything raised that doesn't line up cleanly across the record: either point to updated/corrective documentation if one exists for that specific item, or simply say honestly that you don't recall the specific detail because it was a one-time event a long time ago (e.g., "that was 20 years ago and I genuinely don't remember the exact wording/date"). A real memory gap for a single distant event is a normal, credible answer — don't manufacture a more specific explanation than you actually have.

## What NOT to do

- Don't claim symptoms you don't have to hit a specific rating tier (e.g., don't claim ADL impairment, disorientation, or memory loss for names/occupation if that's not genuinely your experience — the existing record wisely doesn't overreach here, and that consistency is a credibility strength).
- Don't minimize out of pride or habit. The instinct to appear composed and capable is itself something to name to the examiner if relevant ("I'm used to holding it together for exams/interviews, but that's not what my average day looks like").
- Don't guess at legal standards or cite regulations to the examiner — describe your actual experience and let the clinical picture speak for itself.
