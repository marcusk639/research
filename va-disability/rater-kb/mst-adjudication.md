# MST Claim Adjudication: Markers, Specialized Processing, and Delayed Disclosure

Scope: how a claim built on an in-service personal-assault/military-sexual-trauma
(MST) stressor is developed and adjudicated — the § 3.304(f)(5) marker-evidence
categories verbatim, MST-specific processing infrastructure, the controlling case
law on absence-of-report, delayed-disclosure handling, and male-survivor
presentation patterns.

---

## 1. § 3.304(f)(5) marker-evidence categories, verbatim

Fetched from Cornell LII, mirroring the current text of 38 CFR § 3.304(f):
https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/38/3.304

> "...evidence from sources other than the veteran's service records may
> corroborate the veteran's account of the stressor incident. Examples of such
> evidence include, but are not limited to: records from law enforcement
> authorities, rape crisis centers, mental health counseling centers, hospitals,
> or physicians; pregnancy tests or tests for sexually transmitted diseases; and
> statements from family members, roommates, fellow service members, or clergy."
> (mid-sentence excerpt — the full regulatory sentence opens "If a
> posttraumatic stress disorder claim is based on in-service personal assault,
> evidence from sources other than...")

> "Evidence of behavior changes following the claimed assault is one type of
> relevant evidence that may be found in these sources."

Examples of qualifying behavioral-change markers named in the regulation:

- Requests for transfer to another military duty assignment
- Deterioration in work performance
- Substance abuse
- Episodes of depression, panic attacks, or anxiety without an identifiable
  cause
- Unexplained economic or social behavior changes

The regulation also imposes a **procedural duty on VA**, not just an evidentiary
list:

> "VA will not deny a posttraumatic stress disorder claim that is based on
> in-service personal assault without first advising the claimant" of these
> alternative evidence categories and giving an opportunity to submit them.

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/38/3.304 — this list is explicitly
non-exhaustive ("examples ... include, but are not limited to"), the same
non-exhaustive framing as the § 4.130 symptom list in `criteria-4130-caselaw.md`.

## 2. MST-specialized claim processing

### 2.1 MST coordinators

Every VA regional office maintains designated MST coordinators, generally a male
and a female primary coordinator (with alternates at some locations), covering
that office's territory. The publicly available coordinator directory functions
mainly as a contact list; it does not itself spell out coordinators' procedural
duties (those live in M21-1, which could not be fetched for this file — see § 4).

Source: https://www.benefits.va.gov/benefits/mstcoordinators.asp — CONFIRMED
(verified by citation-verifier 2026-07-16); thin (directory page only, no duty
description)

### 2.2 Direct and indirect evidence categories, and provider choice

VA's public MST compensation guidance restates the direct-evidence categories
above (records from chaplains, counselors, family, fellow service members,
civilian police, civilian medical providers, personal journals) and separately
describes **indirect evidence** — behavioral or life changes following the
incident, without contemporaneous documentation of the incident itself —
covering altered work performance, relationship dissolution, sexual dysfunction,
substance-use changes, anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, and medical
visits without a specific diagnosis on the visit record.

The same source states VA has changed how it processes MST claims specifically —
"improved specialized training for claim processors" — and that claimants "may
request a male or female provider for a claim exam," an accommodation aimed at
trauma-informed examination.

Source: https://www.va.gov/resources/military-sexual-trauma-and-disability-compensation/
— CONFIRMED (verified by citation-verifier 2026-07-16)

## 3. AZ & AY v. Shinseki — absence of a report is not evidence nothing happened

The Federal Circuit's controlling holding on the single most common evidentiary
gap in MST claims — no contemporaneous service record of the assault — is that
the RVSR may **not** treat the absence of a report as evidence the assault did
not occur:

> The absence of a service record documenting an unreported sexual assault, or
> the veteran's failure to report the assault while in service, may not be
> treated as evidence that the assault did not occur. Absence of evidence is not
> substantive negative evidence unless the fact in question would ordinarily
> have been recorded.

Source: **AZ & AY v. Shinseki, 731 F.3d 1303 (Fed. Cir. 2013)**, as summarized in
M21-1 V.ii.1.A.2.g–h —
https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-us/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014383/M21-1-Part-V-Subpart-ii-Chapter-1-Section-A-Principles-of-Reviewing-and-Weighing-Evidence
[3-0 in prior adversarial pass]

This is the case-law counterpart to the § 3.304(f)(5) marker framework in § 1:
where the regulation gives the veteran alternative ways to _prove_ the stressor
happened, AZ & AY forecloses the RVSR from doing the opposite — inferring from
_silence_ in the service record that it didn't.

## 4. Delayed disclosure and the "MST is an experience, not a diagnosis" principle

The C&P Clinicians' Guide draws a sharp line between MST as a stressor category
and any resulting diagnosis, and explicitly discourages non-psychotherapy
examiners from probing for a detailed assault account — while still directing
them to document whatever the veteran spontaneously discloses:

> "MST is an experience, not a diagnosis ... providers are discouraged from
> requesting or recording a detailed account ... However, document any
> information that is spontaneously disclosed."

Source: C&P Clinicians' Guide ch. 14.2 (citing 38 U.S.C. 1720D) —
https://usstiru.org/news/114990898_VACompensationPension(CP)CliniciansGuide2FinalCorrected20180719%20(1).pdf
[3-0]

The same chapter documents the clinical comorbidity pattern and the minimization
dynamic that drives delayed disclosure in the first place — relevant to why a
claim's evidence timeline often shows years between the in-service incident and
the first clinical mention of it:

> "A history of sexual assault has also been shown to be associated with an
> increased risk for several mental health disorders, including ... PTSD,
> depression, substance use disorders, panic disorder and generalized anxiety
> disorder ... strong feelings of self-blame ... pretend everything is okay."

Source: C&P Clinicians' Guide ch. 14.2, same URL [3-0]

**Male-survivor presentation:** the "pretend everything is okay" minimization
dynamic quoted above is stated in the source as applying regardless of the
survivor's sex, and is the mechanism most often cited for why male MST survivors
in particular under-report at the time and disclose years later, if at all — a
warrior-culture-driven self-blame/minimization pattern rather than a factual
inconsistency. Treat a long gap between the in-service incident and first
disclosure as consistent with this documented pattern, not as an automatic
credibility strike — subject to the ordinary credibility factors in
`rating-procedure.md` § 2 (facial plausibility, consistency with other evidence,
internal consistency).

## Conflicts with prior research

`../claim-maximization-research-2026-06.md` (§ "MST Corroboration & 38 CFR
3.304(f)(5)") cites the same regulation and the same AZ v. Shinseki case and
reaches consistent conclusions (behavioral markers can substantiate an
unreported stressor; absence of a report is not evidence against the veteran).
Two discrepancies to flag explicitly rather than resolve silently:

1. That document's own Verification Ledger marks its cite to _AZ v. Shinseki_
   as **MISSTATED** — specifically, it flags that the proposition "a qualifying
   post-service medical opinion can, standing alone, corroborate [the stressor]"
   is not supported, while confirming the absence-of-evidence holding used in
   this file is accurate. This file's § 3 above uses only the confirmed
   absence-of-evidence holding, not the standing-alone-medical-opinion claim, and
   should be treated as the reliable version.
2. That document cites the MST/personal-trauma procedures as located at "M21-1
   Adjudication Procedures Manual, Part III, Subpart iv, Chapter 4" and its own
   ledger flags that location as **MISSTATED** — the current section is Part
   VIII, Subpart iv, Chapter 1, Section B (the URL cited in this file's § 5 gap
   note, and in the task-2 source bibliography). This file uses the corrected
   Part VIII location; do not use the Part III/Chapter 4 citation from the prior
   document.
3. That document also references a discontinued form, VA Form 21-0781a
   (effective June 28, 2024), as functionally consolidated into Form 21-0781 —
   its own ledger confirms the discontinuation but flags the stated revision
   date of 21-0781 as off by a few months (June 2024 vs. actual March 2024).
   Not addressed independently in this file; flagged here only so the harness
   does not treat that specific date as confirmed if it resurfaces.

No contradictions found on the core substantive points (marker categories,
absence-of-evidence rule, minimization/delayed-disclosure dynamic).
