# RVSR Rating Procedure for Mental-Health Claims

Scope: how a Rating Veterans Service Representative (RVSR) is instructed to
process a mental-health disability claim under the M21-1 Adjudication Procedures
Manual — the evidence-weighing method, the lay-evidence competence/credibility
distinction, benefit of the doubt, the examiner's nexus-probability language, how
self-report/psychometric instruments are weighed, and the structure of the
resulting rating-decision narrative.

---

## 1. The five-step evidence-evaluation method

M21-1 V.ii.1.A.1.f sets out a five-step method the RVSR is instructed to apply to
every claim, and requires the decision to explain _why_ evidence was found
persuasive or unpersuasive rather than simply stating a conclusion:

1. Determine the facts required to decide the claim and the applicable standard
   of proof.
2. Discount immaterial evidence.
3. Assess probative value, including the competency and credibility of each
   piece of evidence.
4. Resolve the relative weight of the evidence (what outweighs what).
5. Apply the found facts to the governing conclusions of law.

Source: M21-1, Part V, Subpart ii, Chapter 1, Section A —
https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-us/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014383/M21-1-Part-V-Subpart-ii-Chapter-1-Section-A-Principles-of-Reviewing-and-Weighing-Evidence

## 2. Face value, credibility, and competence of lay evidence

The RVSR accepts evidence at face value **unless** it is called into question by
other evidence in the file or by sound medical or legal principles. Credibility —
which is a distinct inquiry from competence — is assessed against these factors:

- facial plausibility
- consistency with other evidence of record
- internal consistency
- witness demeanor (where observed)
- interest or bias

Competent lay evidence is limited to matters a lay person can actually observe or
describe (e.g., "I could not leave my bedroom for three days," "I was fired after
a screaming episode at a client meeting") — competence and credibility are
separate gates and both must be satisfied for lay evidence to carry weight.

Source: M21-1 V.ii.1.A.2.b–c, same URL as § 1 above [2-0, 1 errored]

**Do not use** an unverified prior claim that M21-1 lists exactly four
lay-evidence weighing factors (memory accuracy, direct personal knowledge,
recency, competence) — that specific factor list failed adversarial verification
[1-2]. Use the credibility-factor list above instead.

### 2.1 Two case-law refinements on lay evidence

- **Espiritu v. Derwinski, 2 Vet.App. 492 (1992):** a medically untrained
  individual is generally not competent to opine on the medical _etiology_ of a
  condition (as opposed to describing its symptoms); such an opinion, if
  offered, receives little probative weight. Cf. 38 CFR 3.159(a)(2). Source:
  M21-1 V.ii.1.B.2.b —
  https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000014389/M21-1-Part-V-Subpart-ii-Chapter-1-Section-B-Reviewing-Testimony-and-Lay-Evidence
  [3-0]
- **The claimant's own symptom description is still evidence:** despite its
  self-serving character, a claimant is frequently the single most qualified
  source on the disabling effects of their own condition, and the RVSR should
  weigh it as such rather than discount it categorically. Source: M21-1
  V.ii.1.B.2.c, same URL [3-0]
- **38 U.S.C. § 1154(b) — combat-veteran presumption:** for combat veterans or
  former POWs, lay evidence alone can establish in-service incurrence or
  aggravation even absent corroborating service treatment records, so long as
  it is consistent with the circumstances of that service — though a medical
  nexus opinion is still required to connect the in-service event to the
  current disability.
- **Bardwell v. Shinseki, 24 Vet.App. 36 (2010) — non-combat lay evidence:** for
  a non-combat veteran, unsubstantiated lay statements are not, by themselves,
  competent or sufficient to establish an in-service event, injury, or disease
  requiring medical expertise; such statements must be weighed against the
  absence of supporting service records, and VA is not required to accept them
  merely because they are uncontradicted. Source: M21-1 V.ii.1.B.2.a, same URL
  [2-0, 1 errored]

## 3. Benefit of the doubt / equipoise (38 CFR 3.102, 4.3)

Each factual matter needs to be proven only to "at least as likely as not" —
not to a preponderance or beyond-doubt standard. The doctrine is triggered only
at _approximate balance_ between positive and negative evidence — not merely
because evidence exists on both sides:

> "by reasonable doubt is meant one which exists because of an approximate
> balance of positive and negative evidence which does not satisfactorily prove
> or disprove the claim"
> — 38 CFR 3.102, quoted via https://cck-law.com/blog/vas-benefit-of-the-doubt-doctrine/

> "When after careful consideration of all procurable and assembled data, a
> reasonable doubt arises regarding the degree of disability such doubt will be
> resolved in favor of the claimant."
> — 38 CFR 4.3, same source

Two important boundary conditions from M21-1 V.ii.1.A.1.j and A.5.d–e:

- Reasonable doubt applies **only** when evidence is in approximate balance — not
  whenever evidence exists on both sides of a question.
- An examiner's statement that they "cannot render an opinion without resort to
  speculation" is _neither_ a favorable nor unfavorable opinion — it does not by
  itself trigger reasonable doubt in the veteran's favor (**Fagan v. Shinseki**).

Source: M21-1 V.ii.1.A.1.j & A.5.d–e — same knowVA Section A URL as § 1 [3-0]

## 4. The examiner's nexus-probability scale

The C&P Clinicians' Guide (ch. 1.8a, May 2018) gives examiners a controlled
vocabulary for etiology opinions, mapped to rough probability bands:

| Phrase                                               | Probability                                         |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| "is due to"                                          | 100%                                                |
| "more likely than not"                               | >50%                                                |
| "at least as likely as not"                          | ≥50% (equipoise threshold — favors the veteran)     |
| "not at least as likely as not"                      | <50%                                                |
| "is not due to"                                      | 0%                                                  |
| "cannot state without resorting to mere speculation" | use with caution — see § 3 boundary condition above |

Source: C&P Clinicians' Guide ch. 1.8a —
https://usstiru.org/news/114990898_VACompensationPension(CP)CliniciansGuide2FinalCorrected20180719%20(1).pdf
[3-0]

## 5. Weighing self-report instruments and psychometric tests

The controlling principle from the Clinicians' Guide (ch. 14.3b) is that
psychometric/self-report test data can **never** be a stand-alone basis for a
PTSD (or other mental-disorder) diagnosis — it is corroborating evidence only,
to be integrated with clinical interview and record review:

> "Data from psychometric tests never serve as a 'stand alone' means for
> diagnosing PTSD ... The MMPI and MMPI-2 include scales known as 'validity
> scales' that are elevated in people who are trying to exaggerate their
> symptoms."
> — C&P Clinicians' Guide ch. 14.3b, same URL as § 4 [3-0]

The Guide names the Mississippi Scale for Combat-Related PTSD and the PTSD
Checklist as recommended instruments, and endorses MMPI/MMPI-2 validity scales
specifically for detecting symptom exaggeration or an unreliable test-taking
style. Test results are captured in the DBQ Remarks field ("Including any
testing results" — see `dbq-mental-disorders.md` § 1), not in a scored field, and
are then weighed by the RVSR under the same competence/credibility/probative-value
framework in §§ 1–2 above — a self-report instrument does not carry more weight
than a clinician's own findings and cannot substitute for the clinician's
diagnostic judgment.

**Gap flagged, not fabricated:** the verified source material for this KB names
the Mississippi Scale, the PTSD Checklist, and MMPI/MMPI-2 specifically. It does
not independently confirm how DASS-21 or WHODAS 2.0 (both vendor-scored,
self-administered instruments sometimes used in current C&P exams) are weighed
by name. Treat the general non-stand-alone principle above as applying to any
self-report instrument, including DASS-21/WHODAS 2.0, but flag any specific claim
about their differential weighting as unconfirmed until a primary M21-1 or
Clinicians' Guide source naming them is located.

## 6. Structure of the rating-decision narrative

**Primary-source gap:** the controlling M21-1 section (Part V, Subpart iv,
Chapter 1, Section A, "Completing the Rating Decision Narrative" —
https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000180523/M21-1-Part-V-Subpart-iv-Chapter-1-Section-A-Completing-the-Rating-Decision-Narrative
) could not be fetched — the KnowVA site requires JavaScript and returned no
content through automated fetch. The structure below is reconstructed from a
secondary practitioner source and should be treated as **lower-confidence** than
the M21-1-sourced sections above; confirm against the primary M21-1 text before
relying on it for anything but a rough outline.

A VA rating decision narrative has five major parts:

1. **Header** — veteran's name, file number, representative of record.
2. **Introduction** — branch(es) and period(s) of service; which claim(s)/
   appeal(s) the decision addresses.
3. **Decision summary** — what was granted, denied, or deferred, with effective
   dates and evaluation percentages.
4. **Evidence** — everything the rater formally considered; VA practice does not
   require every record in the file to be listed, only that the list is not
   empty.
5. **Reasons and Bases** — the substantive explanation. For a grant: basis for
   the grant, effective date rationale, and symptom-to-percentage mapping. For a
   denial: theories of entitlement considered, why they were not met, and any
   favorable findings preserved for future claims. For a deferral: what
   development is still pending.

Source (secondary, unverified against primary): https://www.veteransbenefitskb.com/ratingdecision

This structure is where the five-step evaluation (§ 1), the credibility analysis
(§ 2), and the nexus-probability language (§ 4) all have to show up in writing —
an RVSR's "Reasons and Bases" section is expected to _apply_ those tools to the
specific record, not just cite them.
