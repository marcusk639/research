The single highest-value action this week is **starting an OSA workup**, not drafting legal arguments. ## The legal framework

Secondary connection runs through **38 CFR § 3.310**: (a) service-connects a disability "proximately due to or the result of" a service-connected disease; (b) extends this to aggravation. The operative three-part test — current disability, service-connected disability, medical nexus — comes from **Wallin v. West, 11 Vet.App. 509, 512 (1998)**; **Allen v. Brown, 7 Vet.App. 439, 448 (1995) (en banc)** confirms aggravation counts "whether or not the additional impairment is itself a separate disease... caused by the service-connected condition." No CAVC, Federal Circuit, or precedential BVA decision addresses OSA-secondary-to-PTSD specifically — this is decided non-precedentially at the Board/rater level under the general framework above.

Three causal pathways, uneven strength. **Strongest/most direct:** PTSD hyperarousal lowers the arousal threshold and disrupts sleep architecture, increasing sensitivity to obstructive events (McCall & Watson, _J Clin Med_ 2022 — peer-reviewed). **Well-established but indirect:** psychiatric medications (SSRIs, SNRIs, mirtazapine, trazodone) cause weight gain, which drives OSA risk — VA General Counsel recognizes obesity as a valid "intermediate step" (VAOPGCPREC 1-2017), extended to aggravation by **Walsh v. Wilkie, 32 Vet.App. 300, 306-07 (2020)**. **Weakest:** a large VHA study shows OSA statistically associated with PTSD/MDD independent of BMI (Babson et al. 2013), but the authors explicitly disclaim causation — use only as corroboration.

Two real BVA grants on this exact fact pattern (Citation Nr. 1648003, 2016; A21018009, 2021) succeeded on **private psychologist/psychiatrist opinions**, not paid IMO firms — the Board called them "highly probative" for citing peer-reviewed literature with complete rationale, and discounted VA's own negative opinions for not addressing the secondary-nexus mechanism at all. A well-reasoned **treating-provider** opinion can realistically be sufficient; a paid IMO becomes necessary mainly as a fallback if that opinion is thin or a VA exam returns unfavorable first.

## Rating

**38 CFR § 4.97, DC 6847** (verified verbatim, unchanged since a 2006 amendment): 0% asymptomatic with documented sleep-disordered breathing; 30% persistent daytime hypersomnolence; **50% "requires use of breathing assistance device such as continuous airway pressure (CPAP) machine"**; 100% chronic respiratory failure with CO2 retention/cor pulmonale or tracheostomy. The 50% CPAP tier is confirmed. Note: a pending, not-yet-final proposed rule (NPRM 87 FR 8474, Feb. 15, 2022) would remove the automatic 50%-for-CPAP trigger — a related SNPRM (89 FR 74162, Sept. 12, 2024) is part of the same rulemaking docket but only proposes an unrelated new diagnostic code (constrictive bronchiolitis), not a DC 6847 change. VA reportedly paused finalizing this rule in Feb. 2026 amid VSO/congressional pushback, with existing CPAP-tier ratings expected to be grandfathered if finalized — not yet in effect, but still a reason not to delay.

## What's missing and the next step

No polysomnography-confirmed OSA diagnosis exists — nothing else matters until that's obtained. A C&P exam does not generate a sleep-study referral on its own. **This week:** see a primary care provider (VA or private) and explicitly report snoring, witnessed apneas, and daytime fatigue to get a sleep-study order. The private route (home sleep apnea test, ~1–3 weeks) is realistically faster than VA referral, and a private diagnosis is legally acceptable as the "current diagnosis" element under 38 CFR 3.159 — GAO reporting suggests VA specialty referrals frequently miss their own 7-day scheduling standard. Once diagnosed, have the treating psychiatrist or sleep specialist write a nexus opinion addressing the hyperarousal/arousal-threshold mechanism directly (not just "obesity from meds"), modeled on the reasoning that succeeded in the two BVA decisions above.
