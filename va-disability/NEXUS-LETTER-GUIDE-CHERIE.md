The iatrogenic fact can still be mentioned as one contributing factor, but it shouldn't be the letter's central theory, because the veteran's own clinical record doesn't support it as the primary driver.

4. **The GAD/MDD theory needs the same nuance as SUD.** Self-reports place anxiety/depression onset around age 18, pre-service — a direct-service-connection theory that ignores this is vulnerable the same way the old SUD theory was.

## What the letter needs to address

### 1. Diagnoses (all five) — and PTSD specifically needs to be an actual diagnosis, not an assumption

State each diagnosis Cherie has independently arrived at through her own treatment of the veteran: **PTSD** (with the in-service stressor), **Generalized Anxiety Disorder**, **Major Depressive Disorder**, **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder**, and **Substance Use Disorder** (opioid/sedative). If her clinical judgment differs from this list in any way — a diagnosis she'd characterize differently, or one she doesn't support — the letter should reflect her actual assessment, not this list.

**This matters most for PTSD.** Across every clinical record reviewed for this claim, no provider has ever formally entered a PTSD diagnosis — it currently exists only as the veteran's self-reported history and personal statement. If Cherie's own clinical assessment supports a PTSD diagnosis, her letter needs to actually state that diagnosis, not just offer a nexus opinion connecting an assumed diagnosis to service. Confirm this directly with her rather than assuming it's already been documented somewhere.

### 2. Nexus opinion — required phrasing, if it reflects her actual opinion

VA requires an opinion phrased at the "at least as likely as not" standard (50% or greater probability) to satisfy the legal nexus requirement. If Cherie's clinical judgment supports it, standard phrasing looks like:

> "It is my opinion that [condition] is at least as likely as not (50% probability or greater) related to [the veteran's] military service."

For PTSD specifically, if her clinical judgment supports it: the stressor is the described in-service sexual assault/harassment (2012, Seymour Johnson AFB) under the relaxed evidentiary standard for personal-trauma claims (38 CFR 3.304(f)(5)) — VA doesn't require an official report, and delayed or non-linear disclosure (which the clinical record shows — see `CLAIM-ASSESSMENT.md` §1.1) is a recognized, common pattern in personal/sexual trauma cases, not evidence against the claim (_AZ v. Shinseki_, 731 F.3d 1303 (Fed. Cir. 2013)).

For SUD, **if she agrees**, the secondary-connection theory (consistent with 38 CFR 3.310's secondary-connection framework) would read something like:

> "It is my opinion that [the veteran's] substance use disorder is at least as likely as not secondary to, and a means of self-medicating, his service-connected psychiatric conditions."

**One nuance for GAD/MDD specifically:** the record contains self-reports placing anxiety/depression onset around age 18, before enlistment. If that's consistent with what Cherie knows from treating him, a pure "caused directly by service" theory may not be the most defensible framing for GAD/MDD — **aggravation of a pre-existing condition beyond its natural progression** (38 CFR 3.306) or a **secondary-to-PTSD** theory may fit the facts better. This is her call based on her own clinical assessment, but it's worth raising directly so her letter doesn't rest on a theory the record could undercut.

### 3. Occupational and social impairment — describe both, not just one

VA's highest rating tier requires **total occupational AND social impairment** — not just inability to work. If Cherie's clinical observations support it, the letter is strongest when it describes concrete examples of impairment in **both** domains: work/occupational functioning (concentration, decision-making, reliability, ability to sustain a work routine) and social functioning (relationships, isolation, family functioning, ability to initiate or maintain connections). A letter that only discusses one domain will map more naturally to a lower rating tier regardless of severity.

### 4. Frequency, severity, duration — not just presence

Under _Vazquez-Claudio v. Shinseki_, 713 F.3d 112 (Fed. Cir. 2013), VA rating levels turn on how often and how severely symptoms occur, not just whether they're present at all. If relevant to her clinical observations, specifics help: how many times a week panic attacks occur, how long depressive episodes last, how often obsessional rituals interfere with daily functioning, whether suicidal ideation is passive or active and how frequently it occurs (_Bankhead v. Shulkin_, 29 Vet. App. 10 (2017) confirms passive ideation alone is a real symptom worth documenting if present).

### 5. OCD-specific content

If genuinely present, obsessional rituals or compulsions that interfere with routine activities are directly relevant — that specific phrase appears in VA's own rating criteria. Intrusive thoughts, checking behaviors, and their functional impact (time consumed, interference with work or relationships) are all worth describing if she has observed them.

### 6. Materials available to review

DD-214, service treatment records (if obtained), the VA Form 21-0781 personal statement, buddy statements from fellow service members (Reyes, Poquette, and Goff once his is obtained — see `CLAIM-ASSESSMENT.md` §1.1), spouse statement, prior treatment records (Starlite, Victory Addiction Recovery, Acadiana Addiction Center, Ocean's Luxury Rehab, Harmony Junction Recovery), and the WHODAS 2.0 functional assessment (once re-exported — the current file is a broken export and needs to be regenerated).

### 7. Two things to flag for her awareness, not surprise

- The treatment record contains instances where trauma history was denied or not disclosed at intake (2015 VA record, 2018 Victory Recovery, several Starlite admissions) alongside one partial, internally-inconsistent disclosure in a 2022 intake. If this comes up, the relevant clinical context is that delayed and non-linear disclosure is a well-documented pattern, especially in male MST cases — Cherie may already be well aware of this literature, but it's worth her knowing the specific pattern in the treatment record before she's asked about it.
- A 2024 Starlite record contains a boilerplate discharge-planning checkbox stating "client is gainfully employed" (accurate at that specific time). His employment situation has changed materially since — this shouldn't be a surprise if it comes up.
- Some records contain self-reports of anxiety/depression treatment and substance use both starting around age 18, before enlistment. This doesn't rule out service connection (aggravation and secondary theories both remain available, see above), but it's worth her knowing this exists in the record before it's raised by an examiner.

## What NOT to ask her to do

Don't ask her to use a specific rating percentage, to describe impairment as "total" if her clinical judgment doesn't support that word, or to omit anything true from her assessment. A letter that reflects her honest, independent clinical judgment — even if it lands at 70% rather than 100% — is far more valuable and durable than one that reaches for language she doesn't actually believe.
