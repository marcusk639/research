## Findings

### A. How fast working depth is actually reachable

**F-W6-01 — Applied research disciplines converged on 1–6 weeks as the floor for decision-grade understanding of an unfamiliar setting, and every one of those protocols assumes a team, not a solo operator.**

- **Evidence tier:** B (methods literature with reported field timeframes; no controlled comparison against long fieldwork exists).
- **Support:** Rapid Assessment Process as applied to clinical informatics reports "a team of at least two individuals" reaching sufficient understanding "in 1 to 6 weeks" to make preliminary design decisions ([PMC2656056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2656056/), sampled). Innovations for Poverty Action's practitioner guide to rapid ethnographic assessment puts the range at four to six weeks ([IPA guide, PDF](https://data.poverty-action.org/assets/files/Version%201.0_Rapid_Ethnographies.pdf), sampled). Both state explicitly that intensive _team_ interaction during collection and analysis is the substitute for prolonged fieldwork, and that RAP "is not intended to replace long-term traditional ethnographic fieldwork."
- **Date sensitivity:** Low. Methods literature, stable.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The timeframes are consistent across independent literatures, but they are practitioner conventions reported in method papers, not measured against an accuracy criterion.
- **Counter-evidence:** These are health/development settings with unfamiliar _cultures_, not commercial domains with published trade literature — a business domain may be substantially faster because far more of it is written down. Against that: the validity argument in every one of these protocols rests on multiple researchers triangulating in real time, which a solo consultant structurally cannot do. No source addresses what happens to rapid-assessment validity with n=1 researcher.

**F-W6-02 — The only quantitative answer to "how many practitioner conversations before returns diminish" comes from qualitative-saturation research: roughly 9 interviews for code saturation, ~12 for thematic saturation, 16–24 for meaning saturation.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (systematic review of empirical saturation tests plus the primary studies).
- **Support:** Hennink & Kaiser's systematic review of empirical saturation tests reports saturation at **9–17 interviews** or 4–8 focus groups, with the caveat that most included studies had homogeneous populations and narrowly defined objectives ([Soc Sci Med](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277953621008558), sampled). Hennink, Kaiser & Marconi separate **code saturation (~9)** from **meaning saturation (16–24)** ([PMC9359070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359070/), sampled). Guest, Bunce & Johnson's original figure was 12. Hagaman & Wutich, replicating across multiple sites, needed 20–40.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high for the numbers; low for transfer. The numbers are robust within qualitative methodology. Transferring them to "how many operators in a client business must I talk to" is my extrapolation, not the authors'.
- **Counter-evidence:** Saturation is itself contested as a construct, and the Hagaman & Wutich result (20–40 across sites) is the direct warning: a client business with several distinct functional groups is a _multi-site_ study, not a homogeneous one, so the 9–12 figure is the wrong end of the range. Nothing in this literature was collected in a commercial consulting setting.

**F-W6-03 — No reliable evidence found on how long it takes a consultant to reach working competence in a new client industry.**

- **Evidence tier:** C (practitioner blogs and forum posts only).
- **Support:** Searches surfaced only management-consulting career blogs and forum threads ([Management Consulted](https://managementconsulted.com/how-consultants-project-expertise-and-learn-at-the-same-time/); [PrepLounge thread](https://www.preplounge.com/consulting-forum/seeking-advice-how-to-ramp-up-consulting-skill-and-sector-knowledge-after-joining-14629)). The only quantified ramp figure surfaced was for sales account executives (~5.7 months to quota), which is a different construct entirely.
- **Date sensitivity:** N/A.
- **Confidence:** High that the gap is real — the large consultancies hold this data internally and do not publish it.
- **Counter-evidence:** None found. This is a genuine evidence vacuum and the single most commonly asserted-without-support claim in the consulting-advice genre.
- **What would settle it:** A firm publishing engagement-level data linking analyst tenure-in-sector to rework rate or client-accepted-recommendation rate. It does not exist publicly.

### B. Primary-source and regulatory landscape mapping

**F-W6-04 — The US federal regulatory corpus is fully machine-accessible through free government APIs, which makes a _verified_ regulatory map cheaper to build than a model-generated one — but the APIs cover only federal law.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (primary government documentation).
- **Support:** eCFR publishes a REST API with search, versioner, structure and admin endpoints ([eCFR developer resources](https://www.ecfr.gov/reader-aids/ecfr-developer-resources), [API docs](https://www.ecfr.gov/developers/documentation/api/v1)) — note both URLs bot-blocked my fetcher on 2026-08-01 and are cited from the eCFR reader-aids index rather than read directly. The Federal Register runs its own documented API ([federalregister.gov/developers](https://www.federalregister.gov/developers/documentation/api/v1)), catalogued at [data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/federalregister-gov-and-api). GovInfo — **read directly on 2026-08-01** — offers an API plus bulk data across CFR (annual editions from 1996), eCFR, Federal Register (2000–), US Code, and Statutes at Large, requires an api.data.gov key, and now also publishes an MCP server for LLM workflows ([govinfo.gov/developers](https://www.govinfo.gov/developers)).
- **Date sensitivity:** Medium. Endpoint shapes and the MCP server are current as of **2026-08-01**; re-check annually. API-key requirement and collection coverage are stable.
- **Confidence:** High for existence and coverage; medium for the "cheaper than model-generated" comparison, which is my inference from F-W6-18 rather than a measured result.
- **Counter-evidence:** The coverage boundary is the finding's own limit. State and municipal regulation, and the private consensus standards that actually bind many SMB operations (ISO, ANSI, NFPA, UL, industry codes), are **not** in these APIs and are largely paywalled. A regulatory map built purely from federal APIs will be confidently incomplete in exactly the places where an SMB's operational constraints usually live — which is a worse failure than an obviously incomplete map.

**F-W6-05 — IRS Audit Techniques Guides are a free primary source that describe, per industry, the business practices, terminology, document flows, income mechanics and interview techniques of that industry — the closest public analogue to a consultant's industry primer.**

- **Evidence tier:** A for what they are and contain (IRS primary documentation); B for the claim that they are high-yield for domain ramp-up (my assessment, corroborated by tax-practitioner commentary).
- **Support:** IRS ATG index page — **read on 2026-08-01** — states ATGs "explain industry-specific examination techniques and include common as well as unique industry issues, business practices and terminology," with "guidance … on the examination of income, interview techniques and evaluation of evidence" ([IRS ATG index](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/audit-techniques-guides-atgs); page last reviewed/updated **2025-11-25**). Roughly 30+ industries/topics currently listed (aerospace, attorneys, child care, construction, entertainment, oil and gas, pharmaceuticals, real estate/foreclosure, retail, and others). A full worked example: [Retail Industry ATG (PDF)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-mssp/retail_industry_audit_technique-guide.pdf).
- **Date sensitivity:** Medium. Index last updated **2025-11-25**; individual guides carry their own, often much older, revision dates.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. The IRS has a direct stake in these being operationally accurate — an examiner acts on them — which is the "stake in being correct" test.
- **Counter-evidence:** Practitioner sources repeat a figure of "about 50" industries; the current index lists closer to 30+, so the catalogue has contracted and secondary sources describing it are stale. Several guides are a decade or more old and describe superseded practice. And the lens is systematically skewed: an ATG documents where _revenue leaks or gets misreported_, not where operations are slow or badly designed — useful for finding money flows, silent on process quality.

**F-W6-06 — No reliable evidence found on the yield of trade-press or practitioner-forum mining as a domain-learning technique.**

- **Evidence tier:** C (no primary literature located).
- **Support:** Nothing methodologically transparent found. The adjacent evidence that bears on it is negative and indirect: models are measurably less epistemically diverse than a plain web search (F-W6-20), which implies that forum/trade material _not_ well represented in model outputs is precisely the material with marginal information value — but no one has measured this.
- **Date sensitivity:** N/A.
- **Confidence:** High that the gap is real.
- **Counter-evidence:** None found.

### C. Expert elicitation, and what AI cannot substitute for

**F-W6-07 — Cognitive Task Analysis gives a documented four-stage structure for extracting tacit operational knowledge from practitioners; its published validation is a usability evaluation, not an accuracy benchmark.**

- **Evidence tier:** B.
- **Support:** Militello & Hutton's Applied Cognitive Task Analysis (1998) defines four stages — task diagram, knowledge audit, simulation interview, cognitive demands table ([Semantic Scholar record](<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Applied-cognitive-task-analysis-(ACTA):-a-toolkit-Militello-Hutton/ebeec8f791051ad4af266ba9739cc3c8cf7eacb6>), sampled). The Navy-funded evaluation taught the method to two groups of graduate students who then interviewed fireground commanders and naval electronic-warfare specialists, and reported the method was easy to use and produced usable cognitive-demand tables. A 2025 review of CTA in organizational research is at [Organizational Research Methods](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10944281241271216) (sampled).
- **Date sensitivity:** Low.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The structure is real and durable; the evidence is about learnability and output usability, not about whether the elicited model is _correct_.
- **Counter-evidence:** The validation design cannot detect a plausible-but-wrong elicited model, because there was no ground truth to score against. This is the same weakness as the SAT literature generally (F-W6-16). Treat ACTA as a good interview scaffold with unproven accuracy properties, not a validated instrument.

**F-W6-08 — Expert networks are a real paid channel into practitioner knowledge and carry documented securities-law and confidentiality exposure; the enforcement record is against the experts and the callers, not the networks.**

- **Evidence tier:** B overall; the enforcement actions themselves are A.
- **Support:** SEC charged six expert-network consultants and employees with insider trading in February 2011 over roughly $6M in illicit gains ([SEC press release 2011-38](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2011/2011-38.htm)). In the Martoma/CR Intrinsic matter the expert-network intermediary (GLG) was not held at fault; the expert and the caller circumvented its compliance procedures ([Integrity Research summary](https://www.integrity-research.com/expert-networks-the-most-lucrative-insider-trading-case-ever/), Tier C secondary). Post-2012 network compliance now standardly includes expert screening, NDAs, call recording and MNPI training ([National Law Review overview](https://natlawreview.com/article/siren-song-securities-perils-expert-networks-risks-insider-trading), Tier B).
- **Date sensitivity:** Medium. Enforcement posture and network compliance practice evolve; figures above are 2011–2013 vintage.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high.
- **⚠ Client-safe flag:** Two distinct exposures, and they run in opposite directions. (1) _Inbound_ — paying a practitioner from Company X to explain "how this industry works" risks receiving that company's confidential or material non-public information, which the consultant then holds. (2) _Outbound_ — a consultant who has taken an expert-network call while holding a client engagement in the same industry has created a channel through which client-confidential material can leak. Nothing found addresses case (2) specifically; it is the operator's more likely risk and is unaddressed in the literature.

**F-W6-09 — The information a domain ramp-up most needs — how this specific client's work actually gets done — is by construction not in any model's training data, and models are measurably narrower than web search on the material that _is_ published.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (for the diversity measurement); B (for the scope claim, which is definitional).
- **Support:** See F-W6-20. The scope claim itself is not contestable: tacit, local, unpublished operational knowledge cannot be retrieved from a corpus that does not contain it.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low for the scope claim.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Counter-evidence:** The boundary is softer than it looks. A large share of what feels like tacit local knowledge is actually generic-industry knowledge the practitioner has never seen written down, and models handle that well. No source quantifies the split, so the practical question — what fraction of a diagnosis rests on genuinely local knowledge — is open.

### D. Calibration — do you understand the domain, or do you just talk like it?

This is the section with the strongest evidence in the workstream. Four distinct, independently
validated instruments exist. None of them is "read more."

**F-W6-10 — Forcing yourself to write a step-by-step causal explanation is a validated detector of exactly the failure mode in question, and it is selective: it exposes shallow _mechanistic_ understanding while leaving factual, procedural and narrative knowledge undisturbed.**

- **Evidence tier:** A. **Read in full.**
- **Support:** Rozenblit & Keil, "The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth," Cognitive Science 26(5), 2002 — 12 studies, participants per study ranging 12–54, Yale plus one regional-university replication ([full text, PMC3062901](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/); [publisher record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1); 898 citations). Participants rated their understanding 1–7, then wrote a detailed mechanistic explanation, then re-rated. Drop from initial rating to post-explanation rating: **0.918 points for devices**, **0.860 for natural phenomena**, **0.291 for facts** (a significantly smaller drop, TIME × DOMAIN _F_(1,147) = 15.471, _p_ < .001, η² = .095), **0.351 for narratives** (not significant), and **−0.173 for procedures** — i.e. no illusion at all (_F_(2,70) = 1.598, _p_ = .210; devices-vs-procedures interaction _F_(1,132) = 40.934, η² = .237). Combined-studies repeated-measures ANOVA on time: _F_(4,188) = 44.11, _p_ < .001, η² = .448.
- **Date sensitivity:** None.
- **Confidence:** High. Twelve internal studies, a non-elite replication, alternative explanations (social desirability) explicitly ruled out in Study 11.
- **Counter-evidence:** Three real limits, all stated by the authors. (1) The dependent measure is a _self-rating_, not performance against a normative standard — the studies show confidence collapsing, not knowledge being measured. (2) Samples are undergraduate/graduate at selective institutions. (3) Study 6 found that explicitly warning participants about the illusion reduced but did not eliminate it — so knowing about the effect does not immunize you. Note also the asymmetry cuts both ways: because procedures show _no_ illusion, being able to recite a client's process steps is not evidence you understand why the process behaves as it does.

**F-W6-11 — Whether domain intuition can be trusted at all is a property of the environment, not of the analyst: it requires a high-validity environment plus rapid, unambiguous feedback. Consulting diagnosis has neither.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (peer-reviewed theoretical synthesis by the two principals of the opposing research programs; not an experiment). Sampled, not read in full — the primary is paywalled.
- **Support:** Kahneman & Klein, "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree," _American Psychologist_ 64(6):515–526, [doi:10.1037/a0016755](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755). The two conditions: an environment regular enough to be predictable, and an opportunity to learn those regularities through practice with high-quality feedback. Their explicit conclusion is that evaluating an intuitive judgment requires assessing _the predictability of the environment_ and _the individual's opportunity to learn its regularities_ — not the individual's confidence or seniority.
- **Date sensitivity:** None.
- **Confidence:** High for the framework; the framework is qualitative and gives no threshold.
- **Counter-evidence:** It is a synthesis paper, not a measurement. The boundary between high- and low-validity environments is argued by example, and the paper has been criticised for leaving "high validity" underdefined. Applying it to consulting is my extension: a consultant diagnosing an SMB's operations receives feedback months later, confounded by implementation quality and by the client's own actions — the textbook low-validity, delayed-and-noisy-feedback case, which is where the paper says intuition should be least trusted.

**F-W6-12 — Depth of domain expertise did not predict forecasting accuracy in the largest study ever run on the question; cognitive style did. "Understanding the domain" and "being able to predict in it" are separate competencies.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (Tetlock, _Expert Political Judgment_ — 284 experts, 82,361 forecasts, ~20 years).
- **Support:** [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691178288/expert-political-judgment) (sampled). Headline results: expert forecasts only marginally beat chance; the specific domain of expertise had little bearing on accuracy; integrative "fox" style outperformed specialist "hedgehog" style; and greater media prominence was associated with _worse_ accuracy.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low; data collection ended 2003, findings replicated in spirit by the later Good Judgment Project tournaments.
- **Confidence:** High for the result. Medium for transfer to operational consulting.
- **Counter-evidence:** The transfer is genuinely uncertain and I flag it rather than assert it. EPJ's questions were long-horizon geopolitical/economic events in a low-validity environment. "Will this AP clerk's rework rate fall if we change the intake form" is short-horizon, mechanistically constrained, and much higher-validity — precisely the regime where Kahneman & Klein say expertise _should_ work. So EPJ argues against trusting sweeping domain-level pronouncements, not against trusting a mechanism-level operational prediction.

**F-W6-13 — Calibration is trainable and cheap: under an hour of probabilistic-reasoning training produced 6–12% Brier-score improvement sustained across four annual tournaments, and the single principle most associated with accuracy was using comparison classes.**

- **Evidence tier:** A. **Read in full.**
- **Support:** "Developing expert political judgment: The impact of training and practice on judgmental accuracy in geopolitical forecasting tournaments," _Judgment and Decision Making_ (Chang, Chen, Mellers & Tetlock — authorship per the journal page), [full article](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16511/jdm16511.html). Training module "CHAMPS KNOW," designed for under one hour. Improvements over control: **Year 1 10–11%** (n=113–119 trained, _p_ < .001), **Year 2 12%** (n=205, _p_ < .001), **Year 3 6%** (n=97, _p_ = .021), **Year 4 7%** (n=131, _p_ = .028). Training reduced overconfidence and improved both calibration and resolution. Year-4 explanation coding: **C (comparison classes)** mean Brier 0.17 (n=580, _p_ < .001) and **M (mathematical/statistical models)** 0.26 (n=453) were associated with the best accuracy. Probabilistic-reasoning principles predicted improvement; _political-reasoning_ principles (the domain-content half of the training) did not. Training benefits did not interact with cognitive ability — they were additive for everyone.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low. Tournament data 2011–2015; the effect has not been re-measured with AI assistance.
- **Confidence:** High. Four independent annual replications with randomized assignment.
- **Counter-evidence:** Three. (1) The effect shrank after Year 2 (12% → 6–7%). (2) Two trained principles were associated with _worse_ Brier scores — "P (post-mortem)" (mean 1.04, likely an artifact of post-mortems following bad forecasts) and, more troublingly, **"O (consider other perspectives)" at mean 0.63, _p_ < .001** — a direct empirical strike against the assumption that deliberately seeking alternative viewpoints improves judgment. (3) All questions were geopolitical; nothing establishes transfer to business-operational forecasting.

**F-W6-14 — Dunning-Kruger is a weak foundation for a calibration protocol: the canonical asymmetric pattern is mostly a statistical artefact of the better-than-average effect plus regression to the mean. What survives is a modest, roughly linear positive correlation between real and self-assessed skill.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (peer-reviewed methodological critique plus an independent statistical replication).
- **Support:** Gignac & Zajenkowski, "The Dunning-Kruger effect is (mostly) a statistical artefact: Valid approaches to testing the hypothesis with individual differences data," _Intelligence_ 2020 ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289620300271); [PDF mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/2020-gignac.pdf) — PDF would not parse for my fetcher, so this is cited from abstract and secondary summaries, not read in full). Independently, [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.840180/full) shows the iconic "scissors" chart can be generated from random data plus regression to the mean and the better-than-average effect. Nuhfer et al. (2016, 2017) reach the same conclusion with better instruments.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. I did not read the primary in full; the convergence of three independent critiques carries the claim.
- **Counter-evidence:** The effect is not fully debunked. Results depend on method: classical analyses support DKE across conditions while more advanced methods do not consistently. Self-assessment accuracy does correlate with actual skill — the signal is real, the asymmetry is overstated. Practical consequence: "as a novice I will not notice my own incompetence" is a weaker prior than usually assumed, which makes _external_ measurement more important, not less.

**F-W6-15 — Teaching the domain back is a real but modest learning intervention (g ≈ 0.48), and only if the teach-back is declared before you study; imposed afterwards, the benefit is zero.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (meta-analysis, 39 studies).
- **Support:** Meta-analysis of preparing-to-teach and teaching, [Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09871-4) (sampled). Teaching after studying **with** teaching expectancy: **g = 0.48, 95% CI [0.34, 0.63]**. Teaching after studying **without** prior teaching expectancy: **g = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.14, 0.11]** — indistinguishable from zero. Pooled across both: g = 0.27 [0.15, 0.39]. Benefits appeared on both immediate and delayed post-tests.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low.
- **Confidence:** High for the effect; low for its use as a _diagnostic_. The meta-analysis measures teaching as a learning intervention. Its value as a test of whether you understand something is untested.
- **Counter-evidence:** The zero-effect condition is the important one and is routinely omitted when this technique is promoted. It also means the popular "explain it to a rubber duck after you've read" version has no evidential support; the supported version requires committing to teach _before_ reading.

**F-W6-16 — Retrieval practice is what makes domain knowledge durable in the operator's head; a written knowledge base is durable on disk and does nothing for recall.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (meta-analysis, 159 studies).
- **Support:** Rowland (2014), "The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: a meta-analytic review of the testing effect," _Psychological Bulletin_ — **g = 0.50**, with **81%** of comparisons favouring retrieval over repeated study ([PDF](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1:EPFL+DEMO+2020+type@asset+block/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf), sampled; [record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-effect-of-testing-versus-restudy-on-retention:-Rowland/5d4dd1c73554ff1c5c493c2795aadd5aa8bfda17)). Effects are larger when material is complex, when retrieval is effortful (recall > recognition), when feedback is given, and when initial retrieval success exceeds ~75%.
- **Date sensitivity:** None.
- **Confidence:** High for the effect. Low-medium for transfer: the studies use educational materials, not professional domain models.
- **Counter-evidence:** Predominantly lab studies with student populations and verbal materials. Also worth noting against the operator's likely instinct: nothing here says a _written_ knowledge base helps the author remember. It helps retrieval only if the author is subsequently tested against it.

**F-W6-17 — The structured-analytic-technique family — the intelligence-community and consulting toolkit of formal analysis methods — has never been subjected to sustained validation, so its methods should be treated as scaffolding, not as evidence.**

- **Evidence tier:** A/B (peer-reviewed critique in a specialist journal; the critique itself is argumentative rather than experimental).
- **Support:** Chang, Berdini, Mandel & Tetlock, "Restructuring structured analytic techniques in intelligence," _Intelligence and National Security_ 33(3), 2018 ([publisher record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02684527.2017.1400230) — abstract elided by publisher and my fetcher was blocked; cited from the [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Restructuring-structured-analytic-techniques-in-Chang-Berdini/363817aa1ba6f47254737b61c0ec159c3ea3d623) and secondary summaries). Core claim: SATs "have not been subject to sustained scientific scrutiny that could reveal when they are helping or harming"; a specific diagnosed flaw is that SATs treat bipolar biases as unipolar — i.e. they push analysts in one direction on a bias that can err either way, and can therefore overcorrect.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low. I found no evidence the validation gap has since been closed.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high for the gap; medium for the specific bipolar-bias mechanism, which I could not read in the primary.
- **Counter-evidence:** ICD 203 has been shown to support reasonably reliable _inter-rater evaluation_ of analytic products (15 raters, 64 reports) — so the standards produce consistent scoring even if the techniques themselves are unvalidated. Consistency is not accuracy. A defence of SATs on pragmatic grounds appears in [Int'l Journal of Intelligence and CounterIntelligence 37(2)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08850607.2023.2241308) (sampled).

**F-W6-18 — A free, government-maintained standard exists for what an analytic product must carry — sourcing quality, explicit uncertainty language, alternatives considered, and changes from prior judgments — and it is directly reusable as the required-fields schema for a domain knowledge-base entry.**

- **Evidence tier:** B (primary directive exists and is public; my fetcher was 403'd on the DNI PDF on 2026-08-01, so this is cited from secondary descriptions of a primary document — treat the specific enumeration as unverified until read).
- **Support:** ICD 203, "Analytic Standards," originally issued 2007, most recently amended **2022-01-21** ([DNI PDF](https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203-Analytic-Standards.pdf); [ODNI objectivity page](https://www.dni.gov/index.php/how-we-work/objectivity)). Five overarching analytic standards and nine tradecraft standards; requires that confidence language be aligned with probabilistic judgments and that alternative explanations be addressed.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low; last amended 2022-01-21.
- **Confidence:** Medium. High that the directive and its structure exist; medium on the exact enumeration, which I did not read in the primary.
- **Counter-evidence:** F-W6-17 — the standards govern the _form_ of the product, and there is no evidence that conforming to them produces more accurate analysis. Adopting ICD 203 as a KB schema buys auditability, not correctness.

### E. Documented failure modes of AI-assisted domain learning

**F-W6-19 — Learning a topic from LLM synthesis produces measurably shallower knowledge than learning the same facts from search links, across seven preregistered experiments (n = 10,462) — and the effect survives holding the factual content constant and adding live web links.**

- **Evidence tier:** A. **Read in full.**
- **Support:** Melumad & Yun, "Experimental evidence of the effects of large language models versus web search on depth of learning," _PNAS Nexus_ 4(10), October 2025 ([PMC12560091](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12560091/); [journal](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/10/pgaf316/8303888)). Experiment 1 (n=1,104, ChatGPT vs Google): search time 585.4s vs 742.8s (_F_(1,1102)=44.61, _p_<.001); "learned new things" 3.43 vs 3.86 (_F_=36.04, _p_<.001); advice length 84.6 vs 94.6 words; topical similarity of produced advice 0.159 vs 0.057 (_F_=168.46, _p_<.001) — i.e. LLM-taught participants produced markedly less differentiated output. Experiment 2 (n=1,979) held factual content identical and varied only presentation format; the effects persisted (facts referenced 4.00 vs 4.61, _p_<.001; similarity 0.224 vs 0.072, _p_<.001). Experiment 3 (n=250) reproduced it within Google, standard results vs AI Overview. Experiment 4 (n=1,493): blind recipients rated LLM-sourced advice less helpful (_F_=85.0) and less informative (_F_=103.9), and chose it **24.6% vs 50.6%** of the time in head-to-head. A supplementary replication (n=2,402) added real-time web links to the LLM output and the effect held — **only 26% of participants clicked any link**. SEM supported the pathway: LLM use → reduced search effort → shallower knowledge → less investment → sparser, less original output. The authors report no condition in which LLM learning matched or beat search.
- **Date sensitivity:** Medium. Model generation as of 2025; a 2026-generation model with mandatory inline citations might change the mechanism, though Experiment 2 suggests the mechanism is _presentation format_, not model quality — which would make it robust to model improvement.
- **Confidence:** High for the result as measured; medium for transfer to the operator's case, for the reason in counter-evidence.
- **Counter-evidence:** Substantial and author-stated. Outcomes are self-reported depth plus content analysis of produced advice, **not standardized knowledge tests**. Topics required minimal prior knowledge (vegetable gardening, financial scams). And the authors state directly that the findings "might not generalize … to learning in highly specialized domains" — which is exactly the operator's case. The finding is therefore strong evidence that casual LLM-mediated learning is shallow and weak evidence about deliberate professional domain ramp-up. The 26%-click-through figure is the most transferable part: the affordance to verify is not the same as verification.

**F-W6-20 — Retrieval-augmented generation does not eliminate hallucinated law or regulation. Purpose-built, RAG-backed professional research tools hallucinated on 17–33% of queries, and the dangerous failure is _misgrounding_ — a correct-sounding claim attached to a real source that does not support it.**

- **Evidence tier:** A. **Read in full.**
- **Support:** Magesh, Surani, Dahl, Suzgun, Manning & Ho, "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools," _Journal of Empirical Legal Studies_, 2025, first preregistered evaluation of the category ([Wiley full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jels.12413); [Stanford PDF](https://dho.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/Legal_RAG_Hallucinations.pdf); [RegLab page](https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/)). 202 hand-written queries in four categories (general research n=80, jurisdiction/time-specific n=70, false-premise n=22, factual recall n=30). Inter-rater Cohen's κ = 0.77, 85.4% agreement. **Hallucination rates: Lexis+ AI 17%, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research 33%, Ask Practical Law AI 17%, GPT-4 43%. Accuracy (correct _and_ correctly grounded): Lexis+ 65%, Westlaw 42%, Ask Practical Law 19%. Incomplete responses: 18% / 25% / 62%.** The paper's central conceptual contribution is decomposing hallucination into **correctness** and **groundedness**, and defining a _misgrounded_ response — factually correct but citing a source that does not support it — as a hallucination. Contributing causes coded per system: naive retrieval (Lexis 0.47), inapplicable authority (Lexis 0.38), reasoning error (Westlaw 0.61, Practical Law 0.49), sycophancy (Lexis 0.06, Practical Law 0.03). Longer answers hallucinated more: Westlaw averaged 350 words (SD 120) vs Lexis 219 (SD 114). The authors' conclusion: users "must continue to verify that key propositions are accurately supported by citations," and they name the resulting bind explicitly — verify every proposition and forfeit the efficiency gain, or don't and forfeit competence.
- **Date sensitivity:** **HIGH — data collected 2024-03-22 to 2024-04-22 (Lexis, Practical Law, GPT-4) and 2024-05-23 to 2024-05-27 (Westlaw).** LexisNexis shipped a second-generation product in April 2024 that the study does not cover. Treat the _rates_ as historical and the _failure taxonomy_ as durable. As of 2026-08-01 I found no comparable preregistered re-measurement.
- **Confidence:** High for the taxonomy and the qualitative conclusion; medium for current rates, which are two years stale.
- **Counter-evidence:** The authors' own limitations, which are unusually candid: three products only; a point-in-time snapshot of systems that change under them; 202 queries is small; the benchmark was _designed to be challenging_ and is explicitly not an unbiased estimate of the population-level hallucination rate; and their groundedness coding is a spectrum forced into categories. They also note the tools have real value "when used as the first step of legal research rather than the last word."

**F-W6-21 — Fabricated citations reaching formal filings are now a measured, fast-growing phenomenon rather than an anecdote: a court-verified database passed 1,598 cases on 2026-06-09 and was adding roughly 8 per day.**

- **Evidence tier:** B (an aggregation database; the underlying court decisions are A). Cited from secondary reporting — the primary database 403'd my fetcher on 2026-08-01.
- **Support:** Damien Charlotin's AI Hallucination Cases database. Trajectory: ~200 cases mid-2025 → 719 by January 2026 → 1,227 by early April 2026 → 1,458 on 2026-05-22 → **1,598 on 2026-06-09** → **1,668 on 2026-07-02**. That is ~8/day in June, up from 5–6/day in April. Inclusion is strict: only decisions where a court explicitly found or clearly implied reliance on hallucinated material; accusations do not count. Majority of tracked decisions involve pro se litigants; sanctions range from $5,000 (Mata v. Avianca, 2023) to $15,000 per attorney (Whiting v. City of Athens, 2026). Sources: [GC AI tracker](https://gc.ai/blog/ai-hallucination-legal-cases) (read; Tier C vendor blog reporting Tier B data), corroborated by [PlatinumIDS](https://blog.platinumids.com/blog/ai-hallucination-crisis-courts-2026) and [HAQQ](https://www.haqq.ai/blog/ai-legal-hallucination-audit); primary database at [damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/).
- **Date sensitivity:** **VERY HIGH.** Counts are stale within weeks. All figures dated inline above.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high for the trend and order of magnitude; medium for exact counts, since every count I could reach was a vendor blog citing the database rather than the database itself.
- **Counter-evidence:** The base rate is unknown — the denominator (total AI-assisted filings) is not measured, so a rising count is partly a rising-adoption artifact and partly rising detection. The pro-se majority also means this is not primarily a professional-negligence signal. Every secondary source I could reach sells AI legal tooling and has an interest in the number being alarming.

**F-W6-22 — Source monoculture is measurable and gets _worse_ with model size: across 27 models, 155 topics and 12 countries, every model produced less epistemically diverse claims than a basic web search.**

- **Evidence tier:** A methodologically (large, systematic, novel metric) but **preprint, not peer-reviewed — treat as A-minus**.
- **Support:** "Epistemic Diversity and Knowledge Collapse in Large Language Models," [arXiv:2510.04226](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04226) (abstract read; full paper sampled). 27 LLMs × 155 topics × 12 countries × 200 prompt templates drawn from real user conversations. Findings: newer models are more diverse than older ones, but **all models are less epistemically diverse than a basic web search**; larger model size _negatively_ affects diversity; RAG improves it but unevenly by cultural context; country-specific claims reflect English-language sources more than local ones.
- **Date sensitivity:** Medium-high. Model-specific; re-check against 2026-generation models.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high. The scale is convincing; the metric is new and has no independent replication.
- **Counter-evidence:** Preprint. "Epistemic diversity" is the authors' own construct and no one has validated that lower diversity means worse understanding — a narrower set of claims could in principle be a _better-filtered_ set. The web-search baseline is also not obviously a gold standard. Combined with F-W6-19, though, the two independent literatures point the same way: model-mediated learning narrows the input distribution.

**F-W6-23 — The model will agree with a wrong premise you carry in from the client, and will state the agreement confidently, because both behaviours are produced by the same training procedure.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (sycophancy: controlled study across three vendors' assistants; confidence miscalibration: peer-reviewed and replicated).
- **Support:** Sharma et al. (Anthropic), "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models," [arXiv:2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) — sycophancy defined as responses matching user beliefs over truthful ones; found across Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta assistants on varied free-form tasks; **matching a user's views is among the most predictive features of human preference judgments**, and both humans and preference models sometimes prefer well-written sycophantic answers to truthful ones — so RLHF actively selects for it. On the confidence side, RLHF/SFT post-training degrades calibration and induces verbalized overconfidence, traced to reward models biased toward high-confidence scores regardless of response quality ("Taming Overconfidence in LLMs: Reward Calibration in RLHF," [arXiv:2410.09724](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09724)). Magesh et al. independently coded sycophancy as a contributing cause in 6% of Lexis+ and 3% of Practical Law hallucinations on a benchmark that deliberately contained 22 false-premise queries.
- **Date sensitivity:** Medium. Both are properties of current post-training practice, not permanent facts; mitigations (PPO-M/PPO-C) exist and may ship.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Counter-evidence:** The Magesh false-premise result cuts the other way and is worth stating plainly: on _false-premise_ queries the tools scored their **highest** accuracy, and Lexis+ AI often corrected the mistaken premise rather than adopting it. So sycophancy is real but not universal — models resist obviously false legal premises better than they resist subtly wrong framing. The dangerous case is the client's plausible-but-wrong framing of their own operations, which no study has tested.

**F-W6-24 — The consultant is at maximum risk from bad AI advice at exactly the moment of ramp-up, because susceptibility to automation bias rises as domain experience falls.**

- **Evidence tier:** A (systematic review, 74 studies across healthcare, aviation and HCI, with meta-analysis).
- **Support:** Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt, "Automation bias: a systematic review of frequency, effect mediators, and mitigators," _JAMIA_ 19(1):121–127, 2012 ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article-abstract/19/1/121/732254), sampled). Erroneous clinical decision-support advice **increased the risk of incorrect decisions by 26%**. In prescribing, **5.2% of correct answers were switched to incorrect after receiving advice**; negative-consultation rates ran 6–11%. Mediators: trust in the system, decision confidence, task difficulty — and **lower clinical experience was associated with more decision switching**.
- **Date sensitivity:** Low for the mechanism; the 2012 vintage predates LLMs, and the effect is plausibly larger with fluent natural-language advice, though that is untested.
- **Confidence:** High for the mechanism; medium for magnitude transfer to consulting.
- **Counter-evidence:** Clinical decision support is a different genre: structured alerts against a patient record, not open-ended synthesis. The review also notes overall performance usually _improves_ with CDSS use — the finding is about a new class of errors introduced, not net harm. Extending "lower experience → more switching" to a consultant in an unfamiliar domain is my inference, though it is the most direct reading of the mediator.

## Gaps

1. **No study measures whether AI-assisted domain learning changes diagnostic accuracy.** F-W6-19 measures self-reported depth and the quality of advice about gardening. The outcome the operator cares about — whether a consultant who ramped with AI assistance identifies the right operational pain point — has never been measured. **What would settle it:** a within-subjects design where practitioners diagnose matched cases in unfamiliar domains after AI-assisted vs source-based ramp-up, scored against a ground truth established by domain insiders. Nothing like this exists.

2. **No validated calibration instrument for "understanding a business domain."** Every instrument in Section D was validated on something else: mechanistic explanations of devices (F-W6-10), geopolitical forecasting (F-W6-13), educational recall (F-W6-16). Applying them to domain competence is extrapolation, and I have labelled it as such throughout. **What would settle it:** cultural consensus theory offers the right formal shape — administer a domain questionnaire to a small panel of practitioners plus the consultant, derive the answer key from practitioner consensus, and score the consultant's "competence" coefficient against it. I could not verify the method's sample-size properties or its use outside anthropology this session (web-search budget exhausted at 200 calls). **This is the single most important unclosed gap in the workstream** — it is the only candidate I found for a _direct, quantitative_ test of whether the consultant's model of the domain matches practitioners', and I could not finish checking it.

3. **No evidence on the objection-anticipation test.** The brief asked about "the ability to anticipate a practitioner's objection." I found no literature that operationalizes or validates this. The nearest validated proxy is explanation-generation (F-W6-10), which tests mechanism knowledge rather than social prediction. **What would settle it:** a study scoring novices' predictions of expert objections against the objections experts actually raise. Not found.

4. **No evidence on trade-press or practitioner-forum mining** (F-W6-06), and none on whether a _durable_ per-domain knowledge base improves the next engagement in that domain. The personal-knowledge-management literature is entirely C-tier vendor and blog material; nothing measures reuse value.

5. **Hallucination rates in professional RAG tools are two years stale** (F-W6-20, data collected March–May 2024) and no comparable preregistered re-measurement has been published. The failure _taxonomy_ — correctness vs groundedness, naive retrieval, inapplicable authority, misgrounding — is the durable part; the percentages should not be quoted as current.

6. **The solo-operator case is unaddressed everywhere.** Rapid assessment protocols (F-W6-01) derive their validity from team triangulation. Saturation counts (F-W6-02) assume a researcher who is not also the person selling the engagement. No source addresses what a single person, who is simultaneously learner, analyst and vendor, should do differently.

7. **Outbound confidentiality risk in expert-network use is undocumented** (F-W6-08). The entire literature is about the caller receiving MNPI. Nothing addresses a consultant leaking client-confidential context through the questions they ask.

## Implications for the Operator

_My inferences, not findings. Phase C composes; this section exists so the reasoning is not lost._

- The calibration evidence points at one cheap, specific, high-yield instrument: **write the causal mechanism, in prose, before the first practitioner conversation.** F-W6-10 is the only finding in this workstream where the validated instrument and the operator's actual failure mode line up exactly — fluent vocabulary intact, mechanism absent — and the effect is selective for precisely the mechanistic knowledge that operational diagnosis needs. Note the corollary from the same paper: reciting a client's process steps is _not_ evidence of understanding, because procedures showed no illusion at all.

- Three findings (F-W6-13, F-W6-19, F-W6-24) converge on the same point from three literatures: **the ramp-up phase is where confidence and competence are furthest apart, and AI assistance widens the gap rather than closing it.** The Goddard mediator — lower experience, more decision-switching — is the mechanism, and it is at its worst on day one of a new domain.

- F-W6-04 and F-W6-20 together suggest a hard rule with a clean boundary: **anything the operator will state to a client as a regulatory or standards fact should come from an API-retrievable primary source, not from a model.** The federal corpus is free and machine-readable; the model hallucinated 17–33% on exactly this class of question even with retrieval. The boundary case that needs a named policy is state/local regulation and paywalled private standards, where no primary API exists and the model's error rate is therefore untested and unfalsifiable.

- F-W6-02's range (9–17, rising to 20–40 across heterogeneous sites) is the only defensible basis I found for **scoping and pricing the discovery phase of an engagement by interview count** — and it argues that a single-function engagement and a cross-functional one are different products, not the same product at different sizes. That crosses into W8's lane; flagging, not developing.

- One counter-intuitive result deserves carrying forward because it contradicts standard consulting practice: in the Good Judgment Project's own data, the trained principle "**consider other perspectives**" was associated with _worse_ Brier scores (mean 0.63, _p_ < .001), while "use comparison classes" was associated with the best (0.17). If that transfers, the highest-value ramp-up move is not stakeholder-perspective gathering but **finding the reference class — other businesses of this shape — and anchoring on its base rates.** Transfer is unestablished.
