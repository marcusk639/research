# Ketamine for Post-Traumatic Anxiety and Panic: Subcutaneous Dosing, Timing, and the Dissociation Question

**Status:** Literature review, complete as of 2026-08-30
**Scope:** Whether ketamine benefits a non-suicidal person in daily anxiety and recurrent panic weeks-to-months after a traumatic event; what subcutaneous (SC) dosing evidence exists; and whether dissociation is required for benefit.
**This is a literature review, not medical advice.** It is written to be taken into a conversation with a prescriber. It contains no dosing protocol and nothing usable as instructions for unsupervised self-administration. Trial doses appear as study parameters — descriptions of what researchers did — not as recommendations.

**What was deliberately excluded, and why it matters:**

- **Suicidality as an efficacy target.** Trials that enrolled people _for_ active suicidal ideation are not evidence about a person with no suicidality. Removing them removes most of what looks like "ketamine in crisis" evidence. This is disclosed in full in §A5 rather than left silent. Suicidality remains in scope as a _safety_ signal and appears in the harms sections.
- **Bipolar I as a target population.** Only bipolar II is in scope. Where a trial pooled BD-I and BD-II, that is stated at every use, and a pooled result is never presented as a BD-II result.
- **Violent ideation/aggression** as a target outcome. It appears only if it surfaced as an adverse event.

---

## Bottom Line

**Primary question — can ketamine benefit this person?** _Unknown, and the trials cannot answer it, because they never enrolled this person._ Every controlled trial supporting ketamine for anxiety enrolled people with **chronic, treatment-refractory** generalised or social anxiety disorder, or chronic PTSD averaging 12–14 years since the index trauma. None enrolled someone weeks-to-months post-trauma, in acute crisis, in daily panic. Reasoning from those trials to this presentation is an extrapolation across three separate gaps at once — timing, diagnosis, and chronicity. It may be a reasonable clinical extrapolation; it is not a supported finding, and a prescriber proposing it should be understood to be extrapolating.

**Q1 — Does it work for this presentation, and is the timing right?**

- _Does it work for post-traumatic anxiety and panic?_ **Unstudied** for panic disorder (no controlled trial). **Supported** for chronic refractory generalised and social anxiety at SC doses (two small blinded studies, same research group, disclosed commercial interest). **Supported** for chronic PTSD by IV route (two RCTs, both trauma >10 years old).
- _Is the timing right?_ **Contested, and the conflict is unresolved.** Three retrospective cohorts point in three directions: protective (McGhee 2008), null (McGhee 2014; Mion 2017), and harmful (Schönenberg 2005, 2008). The harm signals come from the earliest window (hours-to-days post-trauma). **No controlled trial has ever deliberately dosed ketamine in TR1–TR3 for psychiatric benefit.** The entire early-window literature is incidental — analgesia and anaesthesia cohorts examined retrospectively. The reader at 1–3 months out (TR3) sits in a window with _no_ direct evidence in either direction.

**Q2 — Subcutaneous dose-response.** Conditional on the population being treatment-resistant depression, **the SC threshold for a demonstrated antidepressant effect over an active comparator sits above a fixed 0.5 mg/kg.** The single phase 3 SC trial (KADS, n=174) **failed at fixed 0.5 mg/kg** (remission 6.3% v. 8.8%, OR 1.3, 95% CI 0.2–8.2, P=0.76) and succeeded only when doses were escalated to 0.5–0.9 mg/kg (19.6% v. 2.0%, OR 12.1, 95% CI 2.1–69.2, P=0.005). For anxiety, SC 0.25 mg/kg produced only minor changes; 0.5–1 mg/kg produced response. Physiological and dissociative effects rise across the same range — the curves are roughly parallel, not separated. **Supported** for the antidepressant threshold; **Suggestive** for the anxiolytic threshold.

**Q3 — Is dissociation required?** **No — dissociation is not established as necessary for antidepressant benefit, and the claim that it is rests on a contested correlation that has failed to replicate in most attempts.** Label: **Supported** (for "not necessary"), with the important caveat that "dissociation is irrelevant" is _also_ stronger than the evidence. For **anxiolytic** benefit specifically, the mediation question has **never been asked**: **Unstudied.** And the PK hypothesis in the primary question — that SC's flatter concentration curve could deliver benefit with less dissociation — is **mechanistically plausible and directly untested.** The one population PK/PD model of SC ketamine (Abuhelwa 2022) modelled depression scores, blood pressure and heart rate. It did not model dissociation at all.

## How to read this

Every finding below carries tags: `[TR · PR · R · D · P · T]`.

**Trauma recency (TR)** — the timing axis the primary question turns on.

| Tag | Window     | Territory                                          |
| --- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| TR1 | < 72 h     | Acute stress reaction; memory consolidating        |
| TR2 | 3 d – 1 mo | Acute stress disorder window                       |
| TR3 | 1 – 3 mo   | Early PTSD / subacute                              |
| TR4 | > 3 mo     | Chronic PTSD — where nearly all RCT evidence lives |

**TR4 evidence does not transfer to TR1–TR3.** The chronic-PTSD RCTs enrolled people more than a decade out.

**Presentation (PR):** PR1 daily generalised anxiety · PR2 recurrent panic / panic disorder · PR3 hyperarousal, re-experiencing, intrusion · PR4 accompanying depressive symptoms · PR5 chronic psychosocial instability.

**Route (R):** **R-SC** subcutaneous racemic (primary subject) · R-IV intravenous racemic (reference/comparator) · R-IM intramuscular · R-IN intranasal esketamine (comparator only) · R-SL sublingual/oral incl. at-home telehealth (comparator + risk).

**Dose bands (D):** D1 < 0.1 mg/kg · D2 0.1–0.4 · D3 ~0.5 · D4 0.75–1.0 · D5 > 1.0. Rows populated only by IV data are marked `[IV-derived]`.

**Population (P):** P1 healthy volunteers · P2 primary anxiety disorders · P3 treatment-resistant unipolar MDD · P4 **bipolar II only** · P5 mixed features / activated presentation of uncertain polarity · P6 PTSD and acute stress disorder.

**Time window (T):** T1 0–90 min (the drug experience) · T2 2–24 h (peak therapeutic effect) · T3 2–7 d · T4 > 7 d.

**T1 and T2 frequently point in opposite directions.** Ketamine is commonly anxiogenic during the session and anxiolytic a day later. For someone in daily panic, T1 is a central decision input, not a footnote.

> **⚠ Sedation firewall.** Procedural and agitation-management ketamine (D5, including 4–5 mg/kg IM) is a different intervention, at a different dose, for a different purpose. It appears nowhere in this report's dose reasoning and cannot inform any anxiolytic or antidepressant claim.

> **⚠ Route-bioavailability warning.** Do not assume mg-for-mg equivalence across routes. The only population PK study to model SC, IM and IV racemic ketamine together in depressed patients estimated **SC bioavailability at ~64%** — _not_ the ~90% figure that circulates in review articles. See §B1.

# Q1 — Post-traumatic anxiety and panic

## A1. The peri-traumatic timing problem

**No controlled trial has ever administered ketamine in TR1, TR2, or TR3 with a psychiatric benefit as the intended endpoint.** Every study bearing on the early window is a retrospective analysis of people who received ketamine as an _analgesic or anaesthetic_ for physical injury, later assessed for stress symptoms. That is the single most important sentence in this section, and it should be read before any of the numbers below.

**Why timing is theoretically live.** Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist. NMDA-receptor signalling is required for fear-memory consolidation, extinction learning, and reconsolidation. Preclinical work shows the direction of effect depends sharply on _when_ the antagonist arrives relative to the memory event: MK-801 given 40 min before or 4 h after extinction training impaired the initiation and consolidation of extinction memory, whereas the same drug at 12 h after — once consolidation had stabilised — did not affect the established extinction memory. A drug that blunts consolidation could in principle weaken a traumatic memory (helpful) or weaken the _corrective_ learning that follows it (harmful). The reconsolidation window in humans is on the order of ~10 min to ~10 h after memory retrieval. This is a real mechanistic concern, not a hypothetical one — but it is mechanistic reasoning, and the human data do not resolve which way it cuts. `[TR1–TR3 · PR3 · R-IV · D3 · P6 · T2–T4]` — **Contested at the mechanism level; Unstudied at the trial level.**

**The three human cohorts, in three directions:**

| Study                                           | Design                                                                                        | n                                                               | TR band     | Finding                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Direction      |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| McGhee et al. 2008, _J Trauma_                  | Retrospective chart review, burned US service members (OIF/OEF)                               | 241 total; 147 had surgery, 119 received perioperative ketamine | TR1–TR2     | PTSD 27% with ketamine v. 46% without, despite the ketamine group having **more severe injuries and longer ICU stays**                                                                                                                             | **Protective** |
| McGhee et al. 2014, _Mil Med_ 179(8 Suppl):41–6 | Retrospective, larger burn cohort, same centre                                                | Larger cohort                                                   | TR1–TR2     | **Did not replicate.** No significant effect of ketamine on PTSD incidence; concluded ketamine "does not increase" PTSD                                                                                                                            | **Null**       |
| Mion et al. 2017, _Anaesthesia_ 72:1476–83      | Retrospective, French Military Health Service, all Afghanistan war-injury survivors 2010–2012 | 274 analysed (of 399 survivors)                                 | TR1–TR2     | 98 (36%) developed PTSD; 89 (32%) received ketamine. No association with ASD either    | **Null**       |
| Schönenberg et al. 2005, _J Psychopharmacol_    | Naturalistic, moderately injured accident victims, emergency analgesia                        | 13 racemic ketamine / 24 opioid / 13 non-opioid analgesic       | TR1         | Ketamine analgosedation associated with **increased dissociation, re-experiencing, hyperarousal and avoidance at 3 days post-event** relative to both comparison groups. | Schönenberg et al. 2008, _J Psychopharmacol_    | Naturalistic follow-up, accident victims                                                      | Small                                                           | TR1 → later | Retrospectively assessed acute symptomatology strongly increased in **(S)-ketamine** subjects (dissociation, re-experiencing, avoidance) and slightly heightened in racemic; current PTSD symptoms substantially elevated in (S)-ketamine subjects | **Harmful**    |

**Which side has the stronger design?** Neither, and that is the honest answer. All five are retrospective or naturalistic with no randomisation and severe confounding by indication — sicker, more badly injured patients get ketamine, and injury severity independently predicts PTSD. Mion 2017 is the **largest and the only one to adjust multivariably**, which makes it methodologically the strongest single entry, and it found nothing. Schönenberg's samples are the smallest (n=13 in the ketamine arm) but are the only ones assessing symptoms in the _immediate_ days after the event rather than months later — so they are measuring something the others are not. A recent narrative review (Malik et al. 2026, _J Trauma Inj_ 39:119–127) applying GRADE to this literature rated the overall certainty **low to moderate** and concluded that effects "appear to depend on dose and timing," with perioperative use possibly protective and immediate post-trauma use possibly worsening dissociation and acute stress. That is a fair summary of an unresolved literature, not a resolution of it.

**What this means for TR3 specifically (1–3 months out).** Nothing above was collected at TR3. The harm signal is concentrated at TR1 — hours to days, during analgosedation, in physically injured people, at analgesic rather than psychiatric doses and often in an emergency setting with no psychological support. The mechanistic worry (consolidation interference) is also strongest at TR1 and attenuates as consolidation completes. Whether that worry still applies at 1–3 months is **inference, not evidence**, and my inference — flagged as such, **low confidence** — is that the specific consolidation-interference concern is substantially weaker at TR3 than at TR1, while the _reconsolidation_ concern (a trauma memory retrieved during a ketamine session is momentarily labile) does not attenuate with time and applies at any TR band. What would test this: a randomised trial dosing at 4–12 weeks post-trauma with PTSD-development and anxiety endpoints. No such trial was found in registry searches.

## A2. Anxiety and panic as targets in their own right

This is where nearly all the SC dose-ranging work lives, and it is a small, single-group literature with disclosed commercial interest.

**Glue et al. 2017**, _J Psychopharmacol_ 31:1302–1305. **n=12**, treatment-refractory GAD and/or SAD. Ascending single doses **SC 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 mg/kg** at weekly intervals. Open-label ascending-dose design. Anxiety reduced within 1 hour, persisting up to seven days. A dose-response profile was observed **for anxiolytic effect, dissociative side effects, and blood pressure/heart rate together** — minor changes at 0.25 mg/kg, progressively greater and more durable changes at higher doses. **10 of 12** were treatment responders at 0.5–1 mg/kg. `[TR-n/a · PR1 · R-SC · D2–D4 · P2 · T1–T3]` — **Suggestive** (open-label, n=12, no randomised comparator).

**Glue et al. 2020**, _J Psychopharmacol_ 34:267–272. **n=12**, treatment-refractory GAD/SAD, **not currently depressed**. Double-blind, **psychoactive-controlled** replication: ascending **SC 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 mg/kg** with **midazolam 0.01 mg/kg** randomly inserted into the dose sequence. Improvements in anxiety ratings within an hour, persisting up to 1 week. `[PR1 · R-SC · D2–D4 · P2 · T1–T3]` — **Suggestive** (n=12; blinded and actively controlled, which is a real design strength, but the sample is too small to be more than exploratory, and it is the same group and overlapping patient pool as 2017). **Disclosed interest: Glue and Medlicott hold a contract with Douglas Pharmaceuticals to develop novel ketamine formulations.** Glue is also named on a ketamine tablet patent (Douglas Pharmaceuticals). This does not invalidate the data; it is the reason the data need independent replication, which they have not received.

**Glue et al. 2018**, _J Psychopharmacol_ 32:663–667. **n=20** responders from the ascending-dose study, entering **uncontrolled open-label** maintenance: **SC 1 mg/kg once or twice weekly for 3 months** (data collected Dec 2015–Jun 2017). One hour after dosing, Fear Questionnaire ratings fell ~50%, as did Hamilton Anxiety ratings. CADSS mean scores **declined across the course** — 20 points at week 1 to 8.8 points at week 14 (i.e. dissociation attenuated with repeated dosing). Systolic and diastolic BP rose ~10 mmHg at 30 min. Most common adverse events: nausea, dizziness, blurred vision. 18 of 20 reported improved social and/or work functioning. `[PR1 · R-SC · D4 · P2 · T1–T4]` — **Suggestive** (uncontrolled, open-label, responder-enriched sample: only prior responders were enrolled, so the response rate is not generalisable).

**Lattie et al. 2021**, _J Psychopharmacol_ 35(2). Secondary analysis, **n=24**, of the same programme (ascending SC then 1 mg/kg maintenance). Dose-related reductions in **all three Fear Questionnaire subscales — agoraphobia, social phobia, blood-injury phobia** — and in the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory. The agoraphobia subscale is the closest published signal to panic-spectrum symptoms in the SC literature. `[PR1–PR2 · R-SC · D2–D4 · P2 · T1–T4]` — **Suggestive** (post-hoc secondary analysis of an open/partially-blinded dataset; agoraphobia subscale ≠ panic disorder diagnosis).

**Taylor et al. 2018**, _Neuropsychopharmacology_ 43:325–333. **n=18**, DSM-5 social anxiety disorder. Double-blind randomised **placebo (saline)**-controlled crossover, **IV 0.5 mg/kg over 40 min**, 28-day washout. **Mixed result, and the negative half matters:** significantly greater anxiety reduction on the clinician-rated **LSAS** (Time×Treatment F₉,₁₁₅=2.6, **P=0.01**) but **not** on self-reported **VAS-Anxiety** (F₁₀,₁₄₁=0.4, **P=0.95**). Response over 2 weeks: LSAS 33.3% ketamine v. 0% placebo (z=2.24, P=0.025); VAS 88.9% v. 52.9% (z=2.12, P=0.034) — note the very high placebo response on the self-report measure. `[PR1 · R-IV · D3 · P2 · T2–T4]` — **Supported** for a signal in SAD; **route is IV, not SC.** The saline comparator also permits functional unblinding.

**Panic disorder specifically: `Unstudied`.** No randomised controlled trial of ketamine in panic disorder, by any route, was identified. The published evidence is a single case report of sustained resolution of panic disorder, agoraphobia and GAD after one infusion. Preclinical work is not reassuring: racemic ketamine at sub-anaesthetic and anaesthetic doses did **not** produce acute or sustained anxiolytic/panicolytic-like effects in rats on the elevated T-maze, a model specifically built to dissociate generalised anxiety from panic-like escape behaviour. **For the panic component of this presentation, the honest answer is that nobody has run the trial.** `[PR2 · all routes · all doses · P2]` — **Unstudied.** What would test it: a randomised comparator-controlled trial in DSM-5 panic disorder with panic-attack frequency as the primary endpoint.

**Anxiety inside depression (adjacent, not the same thing).** Ionescu et al. 2014, _J Clin Psychiatry_ 75:e932–8: n=26 inpatients with treatment-resistant MDD, single **IV 0.5 mg/kg**. Contrary to the authors' prediction, patients with **anxious** depression responded _better_ than non-anxious patients, with longer time to relapse and no difference in dissociative or psychotic side effects. `[PR1+PR4 · R-IV · D3 · P3 · T2–T4]` — **Suggestive** (n=26, post-hoc subgroup). This is a signal that co-occurring anxiety does not predict poor response, but it is a study of _depression with anxious features_, not of an anxiety disorder.

## A3. PTSD and acute stress disorder

**Feder et al. 2014**, _JAMA Psychiatry_ 71:681–688. **n=41**, chronic PTSD. Proof-of-concept randomised double-blind **crossover**, **IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg v. midazolam 0.045 mg/kg**. Primary outcome IES-R at 24 h: crossover analysis mean difference **12.7 (95% CI 2.5–22.8), P=0.02**; first-period analysis **8.6 (95% CI 0.9–16.2), P=0.03**. **Mean PTSD duration 14.2 years (ketamine) and 11.9 years (midazolam) — this is squarely TR4.** Trauma types: sexual assault 41%, physical assault 18%, accidents, combat, witnessed violence, 9/11 exposure. Dissociation reported by 27/39 (69%) on ketamine v. 13/31 (42%) on midazolam, resolved by 120 min. Other effects: blurred vision 36%, restlessness 23%, nausea 21%, poor coordination 15%. No emergence of significant psychotic or manic symptoms. **Funding: US Army Medical Research Acquisition Activity. Conflict: Charney and the Icahn School of Medicine are named on a use patent application for ketamine in depression, with a subsequent application for PTSD.** `[TR4 · PR3+PR4 · R-IV · D3 · P6 · T2]` — **Supported.**

**Feder et al. 2021**, _Am J Psychiatry_ 178:193–202. **n=30**, chronic PTSD, randomised 1:1 to **six infusions IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or midazolam 0.045 mg/kg** over 2 weeks. CAPS-5 at week 2: mean total score **11.88 points lower** in the ketamine group (SE 3.96; **d=1.13, 95% CI 0.36–1.91**). Responders **67% v. 20%**. Among ketamine responders, **median time to loss of response 27.5 days** after the 2-week course. Reported as well tolerated without serious adverse events. `[TR4 · PR3+PR4 · R-IV · D3 · P6 · T2–T4]` — **Supported.** (Verification note: I could not obtain full-text CADSS figures, complete eligibility criteria, or the funding/COI statement for the 2021 trial within this review; those parameters are reported here only at abstract level and should be checked directly.)

**Exclusion criteria in Feder 2014 — read this carefully, because it describes what these trials are not evidence about.** Excluded: **lifetime history of psychotic or bipolar disorder**; current eating disorder; alcohol abuse or dependence in the previous 3 months; serious unstable medical illness or sleep apnoea; active suicidal or homicidal ideation; current psychotropic medication use. Inclusion required a **CAPS score of at least 50** — established, severe, chronic PTSD.

**Does ketamine touch hyperarousal and intrusion specifically (PR3), or only global scores?** The published primary outcomes are **global** (IES-R total, CAPS-5 total). Cluster-level breakdowns are not what these trials were powered for, and I found no adequately powered cluster analysis. `[PR3 · R-IV · D3 · P6]` — **Unstudied** at the symptom-cluster level. There is preliminary neuroimaging work (Norbury et al. 2021/2023, single IV infusion, small samples) reporting lower amygdala and hippocampal reactivation to trauma memories after ketamine relative to midazolam — mechanistically interesting, but preliminary and not a symptom outcome.

**What the chronic-PTSD base does _not_ establish.** It does not establish anything about administration at TR1–TR3. It does not establish anything about acute stress disorder — **no trial of ketamine in acute stress disorder was found.** It does not establish anything about a person whose diagnosis is post-traumatic anxiety and panic rather than PTSD. Using Feder to justify dosing someone eight weeks after a trauma is exactly the timing-laundering failure this review was built to avoid.

## A4. The acute session in an already-anxious person (T1)

This is the most decision-relevant tolerability question for someone in daily panic, and it deserves to be read on its own terms rather than as a side-effect list.

**The SC session, from the one adequately-sized SC dataset (KADS, Loo et al. 2023).** Rates in the first ~60 minutes after subcutaneous injection, ketamine v. midazolam:

| Effect                           | Cohort 1 (fixed 0.5 mg/kg) | Cohort 2 (escalated 0.5–0.9 mg/kg) |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Dissociation                     | 48.5% v. 11.4% (P=0.001)   | 77.4% v. 24.5% (P<0.001)           |
| Light-headedness                 | —                          | 79.2% v. 26.4%                     |
| Reduced concentration            | —                          | 73.6% v. 34.0%                     |
| Numbness / tingling              | —                          | 73.6% v. 15.1%                     |
| Dizziness / vertigo              | —                          | 71.7% v. 17.0%                     |
| Confusion                        | —                          | 62.3% v. 15.1%                     |
| Visual changes                   | —                          | 54.7% v. 7.5%                      |
| **Chest heaviness / discomfort** | —                          | **30.2% v. 3.8% (P<0.001)**        |
| Sedation                         | 87.9% v. 82.9%             | 84.9% v. 94.3%                     |
| Nausea                           | 18.2% v. 5.7%              | 18.9% v. 5.7%                      |

`[PR4 · R-SC · D3–D4 · P3 · T1]` — **Supported** (phase 3, active-controlled, prospectively collected safety data).

**Read that table as a panic phenomenology.** Chest heaviness, dizziness, numbness and tingling, confusion, derealisation, visual change — these are not merely "side effects" for this reader; **they are the somatic content of a panic attack.** In cohort 2, roughly three in four participants experienced dissociation and light-headedness, and roughly one in three experienced chest discomfort. Two of the three serious adverse events attributed to ketamine in the trial were psychological: **a major dissociative episode and an auditory hallucination.** Four participants across both cohorts discontinued ketamine for non-serious adverse events, and the listed reasons include **increased anxiety** and **increased depression**.

**Whether that predicts a panic attack in a person who arrives panicking is `Unstudied`** — the trial enrolled TRD patients, not people in daily panic, and did not measure panic attacks. The inference that an already-panicking person is at elevated risk of a distressing session is **plausible and unverified**; I flag it as inference, **moderate confidence**, on the grounds that the drug reliably produces the interoceptive sensations that panic disorder is defined by catastrophically misinterpreting.

**Two facts that cut the other way.** First, in KADS all acute effects **resolved or returned to pretreatment levels within the 2-hour observation period, and no participant required medical intervention.** Second, in the SC anxiety maintenance series (Glue 2018), **CADSS scores fell across repeated sessions — mean 20 at week 1 to 8.8 by week 14** — i.e. the dissociative experience attenuated with familiarity. A first session is likely the hardest one.

**The SC time-course.** SC anxiolysis was reported **within 1 hour** of dosing (Glue 2017, 2020) and BP peaked at **30 min** (Glue 2018). Those are the empirical anchors. **A published human Tmax for SC racemic ketamine at psychiatric doses was not found.** The premise in the primary question — that SC peaks later and lower than IV for the same milligram dose — is discussed in §B1 and is **less well established than the question assumes.**

## A5. What the suicidality exclusion removed — required disclosure

**The published literature on "ketamine in acute psychiatric crisis" is overwhelmingly suicidality research.** That is where the urgency, the emergency-department settings, the rapid-onset framing, and most of the funding sit. Excluding it therefore removes most of what _looks_ like crisis evidence for this reader — and it must be excluded, because trials that enrolled participants _for_ active suicidal ideation describe a different population with different baseline risk, different comorbidity, different care intensity, and a different primary outcome.

**Concretely, what came out:** the esketamine acute-suicidality programme (ASPIRE I/II and the SUI3001/SUI3002 registration trials); ketamine-versus-midazolam trials in suicidal depressed inpatients; and — relevant to Phase 4 — **Grunebaum et al. 2017**, a parallel randomised trial of IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg v. midazolam 0.02 mg/kg in **n=16 bipolar** participants, which was enrolled on the basis of suicidal ideation. That trial is worth naming because its _depression_ outcome was **negative**: depression ratings decreased in the ketamine arm but the between-group difference was not significant. It is excluded as efficacy evidence here under the population rule, and it is not being quietly substituted for the missing crisis data.

**What actually remains as "crisis" evidence for a non-suicidal person in post-traumatic anxiety and panic: essentially nothing.** No trial. The remaining evidence base is (a) chronic refractory anxiety, (b) chronic PTSD, (c) treatment-resistant depression, and (d) retrospective peri-traumatic analgesia cohorts. That is not "no evidence" through a search failure; it is a genuinely empty cell.

**Suicidality remains in scope as a safety signal — see the harms subsection below.**

## Harms, at the same depth as benefits

**Treatment-emergent suicidal ideation.** Reported as a treatment-emergent psychiatric/psychotomimetic adverse event across the ketamine and esketamine trial literature (such events reported in 27 of 33 ketamine studies and 13 of 14 esketamine studies in one safety review). In esketamine registration data, serious treatment-emergent events in the 84 mg arm included suicidal ideation in 2 participants; in one placebo-controlled comparison, "depression suicidal" occurred in 1/112 (0.89%) placebo v. 2/113 (1.77%) esketamine 84 mg, while "suicidal ideation" occurred in 2/112 (1.79%) placebo v. 0/113 (0.00%) esketamine. **Rates are low, directionally inconsistent, and not powered to detect a subgroup signal.** In KADS, the three suicide-related serious adverse events (one attempt in each cohort, one episode of increased suicidal ideation) all occurred in the **midazolam** arm and were adjudicated unrelated. `[R-IV/R-IN/R-SC · D3–D4 · P3]` — **Suggestive** (no established signal of worsening; also no adequate power to exclude one).

**Boxed-warning language.** The FDA label for SPRAVATO (esketamine nasal spray, CIII) carries a boxed warning for **sedation; dissociation; respiratory depression; abuse and misuse**, and — as with all antidepressants — **increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors in pediatric and young adult patients**, with a requirement for close monitoring for clinical worsening and emergence of suicidal thoughts and behaviors. It is available only through a restricted REMS programme. Trial rates in the label: sedation 48–61%, loss of consciousness 0.3–0.4%, dissociative/perceptual changes 61–84%. **Patients must be monitored for at least 2 hours per session.** This is the intranasal esketamine label, not a racemic-SC label — there is no FDA-approved racemic ketamine product for psychiatric use, and therefore no boxed warning specific to SC racemic ketamine.

**Urinary tract.** Ketamine-associated uropathy — reduced bladder storage, frequency, urgency, dysuria, haematuria, suprapubic pain — is **dose- and duration-dependent** and characteristically appears after prolonged high-frequency exposure, typically more than a year of heavy non-therapeutic use. In supervised therapeutic use the risk appears low: large provider surveys and a 2023 case report describe **one confirmed published case of cystitis attributed to therapeutic ketamine for depression**. Mild symptoms are usually reversible if treatment stops promptly. `[R-any · D3–D4 · P3 · T4]` — **Suggestive** (low therapeutic risk; the evidence base is surveys and case reports, not surveillance cohorts).

**Hepatobiliary.** Across RCTs, 75 hepatic adverse events have been reported, predominantly **mild and transient aminotransferase elevations**, with rare liver impairment and **no cases meeting Hy's Law criteria**. Nearly all documented cases of clinically significant hepatobiliary injury occurred at cumulative exposures far exceeding psychiatric dosing (prolonged infusions, repeated courses within short windows, chronic pain protocols). `[R-any · D3–D4 · P3 · T4]` — **Supported.**

**Cardiovascular.** SC dosing raised systolic and diastolic BP by roughly **10 mmHg at 30 min** (Glue 2018, n=20). In KADS, blood-pressure increases were among the acute effects that resolved within the 2-hour observation window. In the population PK/PD model, blood pressure and heart rate followed **immediate-effect models** driven by ketamine concentration, with EC₅₀ values of 468 ng/mL (BP) and 7,580 ng/mL (HR).

# Q2 — Subcutaneous dosing

## B1. SC pharmacokinetics versus IV

**Abuhelwa et al. 2022**, _Clin Pharmacol Ther_ 112:720–729. This is the foundational source for everything below and the only study to model **IV, SC and IM racemic ketamine together in the same psychiatric population**. n=**21** treatment-refractory depressed participants, dose titration **0.1–0.5 mg/kg as single doses**, nonlinear mixed-effects population PK/PD modelling.

Findings:

- Ketamine and norketamine PK best described by **two-compartment models with first-order absorption** after SC and IM administration.
- **Estimated absolute bioavailability after SC and IM ≈ 64%**, with **indistinguishable first-order absorption rate constants** between the two routes.
- Allometric scaling on body weight improved fit for all clearances and volumes.
- **MADRS response was best described by a turnover model with a turnover time of ~42 hours** — i.e. the therapeutic effect is _delayed and slow-resolving_, not concentration-tracking.
- **BP and HR were best described by immediate-effect models** — i.e. they _do_ track concentration in real time.
- For all pharmacodynamic effects, **ketamine alone outperformed models that linked norketamine concentration to effect.**
- Estimated EC₅₀: **MADRS 0.44 ng/mL; BP 468 ng/mL; HR 7,580 ng/mL.**

**The two numbers that matter most.** First, **SC bioavailability is ~64%, not ~90%.** Reviews that enumerate route bioavailabilities frequently omit SC entirely — Ragnhildstveit et al. 2023 lists IV 100%, IM 93%, IN 45%, PO 20% and does not list SC at all — and where a high SC figure is quoted, I could not trace it to a psychiatric-dose human PK study. If ~64% is right, a given mg/kg SC dose delivers roughly two-thirds the systemic exposure of the same IV dose, which is a meaningful part of why KADS needed to escalate.

**Second, and more interesting: the antidepressant EC₅₀ is roughly a thousandfold below the cardiovascular EC₅₀.** That is a formal, model-based separation of the therapeutic concentration-response from the concentration-driven physiological one. It is the strongest quantitative support in this review for the general shape of the primary question's hypothesis.

**What this study does _not_ establish, and it is critical:** it **did not model dissociation.** MADRS, BP and HR were the pharmacodynamic endpoints. There is no CADSS concentration-response curve here. So the model tells us therapeutic effect is decoupled from _haemodynamic_ effect — it says nothing directly about whether it is decoupled from _dissociative_ effect.

**On the "SC peaks later and lower than IV" premise.** SC absorption is first-order, which necessarily produces a lower and later peak than a bolus, and SC and IM absorption rate constants were indistinguishable. But **a published human Tmax value for SC racemic ketamine at psychiatric doses was not located in this review.** For IM, reported Tmax across doses 0.1–6.0 mg/kg is roughly 10–30 min and appears uncorrelated with dose; since SC and IM absorption constants were indistinguishable in Abuhelwa, SC Tmax is _probably_ in a similar range — that is inference from the model, **moderate confidence**, not a measured SC figure. **Do not treat the premise as established.** `[R-SC v. R-IV · D2–D3 · P3]` — **Supported** for bioavailability and PD structure; **Unstudied** for a directly measured SC Tmax/Cmax comparison against IV at matched doses.

## B2. SC dose-ranging evidence

**KADS — Loo et al. 2023**, _Br J Psychiatry_ 223:533–541. The only **phase 3** trial of SC racemic ketamine. Seven mood-disorder centres in Australia and New Zealand. Double-blind, active-controlled, parallel-group. Participants: age ≥18, MDD of ≥3 months, insufficient response to ≥2 adequate antidepressant trials, MADRS ≥20, concurrent antidepressants at stable dose. **Twice-weekly SC injections into the abdominal wall for 4 weeks.** 1,033 assessed for eligibility; 184 randomised; 174 received ≥1 dose. Primary outcome: remission (MADRS ≤10) 3–4 days after the final treatment.

**Cohort 1 — fixed dose. This arm failed, and naming it is the point of this section.**

- Ketamine **0.5 mg/kg** v. midazolam 0.025 mg/kg, identical injection volume. n=68 mITT (33 ketamine / 35 midazolam).
- **Remission 6.3% v. 8.8%; OR 1.3, 95% CI 0.2–8.2, P=0.76. No separation.**
- Response (≥50% MADRS improvement) **9.4% v. 8.8%, P=0.39. No separation.**
- MADRS change **−4.28 v. −3.94. No separation.**
- The Data Safety Monitoring Board reviewed the first 51 completers, observed that **no participant in the entire masked sample had remitted** while the safety profile was good, and recommended revisiting dosage. No interim efficacy analysis was conducted.

**Cohort 2 — flexible, response-guided dose escalation.**

- Ketamine **0.5 → 0.6 → 0.75 → 0.9 mg/kg** (escalating at sessions 2, 4 and 6 if MADRS had not improved by 50%) v. midazolam 0.025 → 0.03 → 0.0375 → 0.045 mg/kg, volumes matched at each level. n=106 mITT (53/53).
- **Remission 19.6% v. 2.0%; OR 12.1, 95% CI 2.1–69.2, P=0.005.**
- Response **29.4% v. 4.1%; OR 12.25, P=0.001.**
- Remission at MADRS ≤12 (for comparability with the esketamine registration trials) **21.6% v. 4.1%; OR 8.26, P=0.007.**
- MADRS change **−8.18 v. −3.45**; difference **5.5 points, 95% CI 2.1–8.7, P=0.001.**
- **Most participants reached the top dose: 30/53 ketamine and 38/53 midazolam were escalated to the highest level.** The effective regimen is therefore substantially a ~0.9 mg/kg regimen, not a 0.5 mg/kg one.
- 24% of the sample had failed ECT.
- Funding: Australian NHMRC APP1105089. Multiple authors disclose consulting/advisory relationships with Douglas Pharmaceuticals, Janssen, Servier, Lundbeck, Otsuka and others; one author discloses equity in ketamine-adjacent companies.

`[R-SC · D3 (failed) → D4 (separated) · P3 · T2–T4]` — **Supported.**

**George et al. 2017**, _Am J Geriatr Psychiatry_. n=**16**, age >60, MDD or bipolar depression, insufficient response to ≥1 treatment in the current episode, MADRS ≥20. Randomised double-blind multiple-crossover, midazolam-controlled, followed by open-label extension. **Weekly ascending SC doses 0.1 → 0.5 mg/kg**, escalating 0.1 mg/kg per session until remission. **MADRS decreased significantly versus midazolam at 0.2 mg/kg (P<0.01), 0.3 mg/kg (P<0.001) and 0.4 mg/kg (P<0.001) — but NOT at 0.1 mg/kg (P=0.06).** Seven of 16 (43.75%) remitted, at doses 0.1 (n=1), 0.3 (n=1), 0.4 (n=3), 0.5 (n=2). **Seven of 16 received up to 0.5 mg/kg and did not remit.** `[R-SC · D2–D3 · P3 (+ some bipolar) · T2–T3]` — **Suggestive** (n=16, elderly, mixed unipolar/bipolar, ascending-dose design confounds dose with session number and cumulative exposure).

**Loo et al. 2016**, _Acta Psychiatr Scand_. n=**15** randomised across routes: **IV n=4, IM n=5, SC n=6**, dose titration 0.1–0.5 mg/kg with midazolam active placebo. Response/remission 75% IV, 60% IM, **100% SC (6/6)**. `[R-SC v. R-IV v. R-IM · D2–D3 · P3]` — **Suggestive, and the SC figure should not be quoted as evidence of SC superiority.** The systematic review that assessed this trial rated it **high risk of bias** for random sequence generation and allocation concealment, and noted **no strategy was described for blinding the route of administration** — so the route comparison is essentially unblinded, and n=6 per arm.

**Glue 2017 / 2020 (SC in anxiety), covered in §A2:** 0.25 mg/kg produced only minor changes; 0.5 and 1 mg/kg produced response in 10/12. **0.25 mg/kg is a named non-separating dose for anxiolytic effect.**

## B3. The SC therapeutic window

**Antidepressant curve.** The best characterisation is **threshold-like with the threshold above a fixed 0.5 mg/kg for a repeated-dose TRD course**. The direct evidence: KADS cohort 1 at fixed 0.5 mg/kg produced remission in 6.3% — indistinguishable from active placebo — while escalation into 0.75–0.9 mg/kg produced 19.6%. **Confidence in that shape: moderate.** The complication is that George 2017 found separation at **0.2–0.4 mg/kg** SC in a different population (older, less refractory, single ascending doses, remission as the stopping rule). Those two findings are not necessarily contradictory — they may reflect that a less-refractory patient needs less drug, or that ascending-dose designs inflate low-dose apparent efficacy by confounding dose with cumulative exposure and session number. **What is not supportable is a single number for "the SC threshold."**

**Anxiolytic curve.** Evidence is confined to Glue's SC programme: **0.25 mg/kg produced only minor changes; 0.5–1 mg/kg produced response in 10/12; 1 mg/kg was chosen for maintenance.** That is a monotonic-looking curve across three points in twelve patients. **Confidence in the shape: low.** No inverted-U has been demonstrated for either outcome; no trial has tested doses above 1 mg/kg for anxiolysis; and no trial has compared multiple SC doses against a common comparator in a parallel-group design — every dose-ranging dataset here is an ascending-dose or escalation design.

**Do the two curves differ in shape?** They cannot be compared: they were generated in different populations by different groups with different designs. `[R-SC · D2–D4 · P2 v. P3]` — **Unstudied.**

**Where do side effects start to outpace benefit?** In the SC data the **anxiolytic, dissociative and haemodynamic curves rise together** — Glue 2017 explicitly reports a common dose-response profile across all three. In KADS, the dose level that worked was also the dose level with 77% dissociation. **On the SC clinical evidence as it stands, benefit and dissociation are not separated by dose.** That is the empirical answer, and it sits in tension with the PK/PD modelling in §B1. Both are reported; §C4 addresses the tension.

## B5. Practical delivery

In every published protocol identified, **SC ketamine was delivered in a supervised clinical setting** — specialist mood-disorder centres (KADS, seven sites) or a university clinic (Glue programme). Injection site in KADS was the **abdominal wall**. Schedules: **twice weekly for 4 weeks** (KADS, TRD); **weekly ascending single doses** (Glue 2017/2020, George 2017); **once or twice weekly for 3 months** (Glue 2018 maintenance).

**SC is used in these protocols precisely because it avoids IV access** — no infusion pump, no cannulation, less nursing time, lower cost, which is why the systematic review of SC ketamine frames it as particularly relevant in resource-limited settings. **That convenience argument is about the delivery apparatus, not about the monitoring requirement.** The published SC protocols retained the full 2-hour supervised observation window. Nothing in the SC literature supports the inference that a simpler injection implies lighter monitoring, and the acute-effect table in §A4 is the reason why.

# Q3 — Is dissociation required?

## C1. The mediation evidence

**The original positive finding.** Luckenbaugh et al. 2014 conducted a secondary analysis of predictors of antidepressant response to ketamine (**n=108**). Change in **CADSS** score was associated with antidepressant improvement at both **230 min and 7 days** post-infusion. Change in manic or psychotic symptoms and change in systolic BP, diastolic BP and pulse at 40 min were **not** associated with later improvement. Critically, and often dropped when this finding is cited: **although statistically significant, CADSS change explained only a small fraction of the variance in antidepressant response.**

**The replication record, given equal weight.** A systematic review of the eight published analyses on this question found the relationship **mixed: only three of eight observed a relationship** between CADSS or psychosis scores and antidepressant response. Among the three positive analyses, explained variance was **12–21%**. A separate analysis found that "floating" — a depersonalisation-type dissociative side effect — **did not mediate** antidepressant response at 230 min or day 12. A study of 10 patients with treatment-resistant MDD found **no association** between maximum CADSS change and change in depressive symptoms after a single subanaesthetic infusion. Most recently, an analysis of **subcutaneous esketamine** using repeated-measures correlation found **no significant within-subject association** between dissociative symptoms and change in depressive symptom severity.

**The authoritative synthesis.** Ballard & Zarate, _Nat Commun_ 2020;11:6431 (NIMH Intramural): _"Presently, the literature does not support the conclusion that dissociation is necessary for antidepressant response to ketamine."_ They also note three reasons the correlation may be unreliable where it appears: possible functional unblinding; the fact that CADSS was developed to measure dissociation around traumatic-memory recall in PTSD and may not be sensitive to rapid drug-induced states; and evidence that some ketamine-related experiences (altered time and sensory perception, unusual bodily sensations, peacefulness, disinhibition) are **not captured by CADSS at all.**

`[R-IV mostly · D3 · P3 · T2–T3]` — **Contested, with the stronger design on the "not necessary" side.** The reason: the positive finding is a _secondary analysis of a correlation_ explaining a small fraction of variance, while the negative side comprises multiple independent replication attempts plus experimental designs (§C2) that manipulate the variable rather than merely observe it. Correlational secondary analyses are weaker evidence than failed replications and experimental manipulations. **This asymmetry matters practically, because the correlational claim is sometimes used to justify pushing doses into a more aversive range.**

## C2. Designs built to separate the two

**Lii et al. 2023**, _Nature Mental Health_. **n=40** adults with moderate-to-severe MDD scheduled for routine surgery, randomised to a single **IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or saline placebo administered during general anaesthesia**. Single-centre, parallel-arm, **triple-masked**. Because participants were unconscious, they could not experience — or infer — dissociation. **Result: no evidence of an effect of group assignment on MADRS at days 1–3, and secondary and exploratory outcomes did not separate either.** Both groups improved substantially. `[R-IV · D3 · P3 · T2]` — **Supported** as a negative finding, and it is the most direct experimental probe available. It cuts in a direction that is uncomfortable for _both_ camps: it undermines the claim that the pharmacology alone suffices, and it raises the possibility that the conscious experience — or the expectancy attached to it — carries much of the effect. It does not, however, isolate dissociation specifically from consciousness, setting, or expectancy, and n=40 in a surgical population is a narrow base.

**Lanicemine (AZD6765).** A **low-trapping NMDA channel blocker with a fast off-rate** — designed to retain NMDA antagonism while producing minimal dissociation. It showed antidepressant efficacy in three of four earlier clinical studies. Then the **phase IIb trial failed**: n=**302** adults with MDD and inadequate treatment response, randomised to **15 double-blind IV infusions of adjunctive lanicemine 50 mg, lanicemine 100 mg, or saline over 12 weeks**. **Neither dose was superior to placebo** in reducing depressive symptom severity; the placebo response rate at trial end was 39%. `[R-IV · P3 · T4]` — **Supported** as a named failed programme. Interpretation is genuinely ambiguous: it is consistent with "you need the dissociative pharmacology," and equally consistent with "lanicemine is simply a weaker drug at these doses" or "the trial drowned in placebo response." **It should not be reported as proof that dissociation is required.**

**Low-dose SC regimens that produced benefit with minimal dissociation.** George 2017 found SC **0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 mg/kg** separated from midazolam on MADRS — doses in the sub-dissociative-to-low range. That is the closest thing in the SC literature to a demonstration that benefit is obtainable below the strongly dissociative range, and it is n=16.

**Active psychoactive comparators.** The consistent use of **midazolam** as an active control — in KADS, Glue 2020, Feder 2014/2021, George 2017 — is itself a design response to this problem: midazolam produces sedation and some subjective drug effect without ketamine's therapeutic mechanism. Ketamine separated from midazolam in KADS cohort 2, Feder 2014, Feder 2021 and George 2017 at 0.2–0.4 mg/kg. Meta-analytically, ketamine-versus-saline comparisons yield a much larger effect (**d=1.8, 95% CI 1.4–2.2**, five studies) than ketamine-versus-midazolam comparisons (**d=0.7, 95% CI 0.4–0.9**, four studies) at day one — quantifying how much apparent effect is attributable to unblinding.

## C3. The anxiolytic case specifically

**Nobody has asked this question. This is the cleanest `Unstudied` finding in the report.**

Every mediation analysis, replication attempt and separation design catalogued in §C1–C2 concerns **antidepressant** response. I found **no analysis testing whether dissociation mediates, correlates with, or is separable from _anxiolytic_ benefit** — not in the SC anxiety programme, not in Taylor 2018, not in the PTSD trials.

What exists is weaker and easily mistaken for an answer: Glue 2017 reports that **anxiolytic effect, dissociative side effects, and blood-pressure/heart-rate change all showed a dose-response profile across 0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0 mg/kg SC in the same 12 patients.** That is three parallel dose-response curves. It is not a mediation analysis, and parallel dose-response is exactly what you would see whether dissociation causes anxiolysis or merely accompanies it. Reporting it as evidence for either position would be overreach.

There is one adjacent data point pointing toward separability: in Glue 2018's maintenance series, **CADSS scores fell from 20 to 8.8 over 14 weeks while patients continued to report anxiolytic benefit and improved functioning.** If benefit persisted as dissociation attenuated, that is suggestive of dissociation not being required for the maintained effect. But this is an **uncontrolled, open-label, responder-enriched series of 20 patients** with no formal analysis of the relationship, and habituation to a subjective rating scale is an obvious alternative explanation. `[PR1 · R-SC · D4 · P2 · T1–T4]` — **Suggestive at most; the mediation question itself is Unstudied.**

**What would test it:** a trial in an anxiety-disorder population with CADSS measured at every session and a pre-specified mediation analysis of session-level dissociation against anxiolytic outcome — or, better, a design randomising participants to a dissociation-blocking pretreatment.

## C4. The PK hypothesis — bringing B1 and C1–C3 together

**The hypothesis under test:** SC administration produces a lower peak plasma concentration and later Tmax than the same milligram dose given IV; if dissociation tracks peak concentration while therapeutic effect tracks total exposure, SC dosing could deliver benefit with less dissociation.

**What supports it.**

1. SC absorption is **first-order** (Abuhelwa 2022), which necessarily produces a flatter, later peak than an IV bolus or short infusion. The _direction_ of the premise is sound.
2. **The therapeutic effect does not track concentration.** MADRS response was best described by a **turnover model with ~42-hour turnover time** — a slow, delayed, exposure-driven process — whereas BP and HR followed **immediate-effect** models that do track concentration in real time. This is a formal, model-based demonstration that at least one class of adverse effect is concentration-driven while the therapeutic effect is not.
3. The **EC₅₀ separation is enormous**: 0.44 ng/mL for MADRS versus 468 ng/mL for BP. Roughly three orders of magnitude.
4. Dissociation is phenomenologically an acute, peak-locked phenomenon — it peaks near Tmax and resolves within ~2 hours (KADS, Feder 2014) — which is the profile of a concentration-driven effect, like BP.
5. Dissociation is not established as necessary for benefit (§C1–C2), so removing it is not obviously removing the mechanism.

**What refutes, or at least resists, it.**

1. **No published human Tmax or Cmax comparison for SC versus IV racemic ketamine at matched psychiatric doses was found.** The quantitative core of the premise is unmeasured.
2. **Dissociation was never modelled.** Abuhelwa's PD endpoints were MADRS, BP and HR. There is no CADSS EC₅₀ and no demonstration that dissociation behaves like BP rather than like MADRS. The inference that it does is **plausible and untested.**
3. **SC bioavailability is only ~64%**, so a flatter curve also means _less total exposure_ per mg — which, if benefit is exposure-driven, cuts against getting the same benefit from the same dose.
4. **The clinical SC data do not show the hoped-for decoupling.** KADS: the fixed 0.5 mg/kg regimen — lower dissociation (48.5%) — **failed**. The escalated regimen — much higher dissociation (77.4%) — **worked**. Glue: 0.25 mg/kg produced minor changes on both anxiolysis and dissociation; response required 0.5–1 mg/kg, where both rose together. **In the actual SC trials, the doses that produced benefit were the doses that produced dissociation.**

**Verdict on Q3, explicit:**

> **Dissociation is not established as a necessary condition for ketamine's antidepressant benefit. Label: Supported.** The claim that it is required rests on one secondary correlational analysis (n=108) explaining a small fraction of variance, which failed to replicate in five of eight subsequent analyses, and is contradicted by low-dose SC regimens that separated from active placebo at 0.2–0.4 mg/kg.
>
> **For anxiolytic benefit specifically, the question has never been tested. Label: Unstudied.**
>
> **The SC-decouples-dissociation hypothesis is mechanistically plausible and directly untested. Label: Unstudied.** Its quantitative premise (SC Tmax/Cmax versus IV at matched dose) is unmeasured in humans at psychiatric doses; its key link (dissociation as concentration-driven rather than exposure-driven) has never been modelled; and the one adequately-powered SC trial found benefit and dissociation rising together across the dose range. What would test it: a PK/PD study modelling CADSS alongside MADRS across SC and IV administration at matched exposure, and a parallel-group SC trial randomising two doses against a common comparator with dissociation as a co-primary.

**The practical consequence, stated plainly and without softening:** on current evidence, a prescriber cannot promise that choosing the SC route will deliver benefit while avoiding a dissociative session. What the SC route reliably offers is no IV cannula, lower cost, a slower onset, and — from the Glue maintenance data — dissociation that attenuates over repeated sessions.

## D2. The mood-stabilizer condition — state this to a prescriber

**Every positive bipolar finding above was generated in patients maintained on a therapeutic-level mood stabiliser.** Diazgranados 2010: participants were "maintained on therapeutic levels of lithium or valproate." Zarate 2012: identical requirement. Across the whole systematic review, **all participants receiving IV ketamine did so "as an adjunctive treatment to a mood-stabilizing agent."** One trial reported the split as lithium n=23, valproate n=13 of 36.

**Consequence:** the efficacy and safety findings — including the low switch rates in §D3 — **describe ketamine given on top of lithium or valproate.** They do not describe ketamine given to an unmedicated bipolar II patient, or to one on an antidepressant, a lamotrigine-only regimen, or an antipsychotic. This is routinely buried in secondary sources, and it changes the interpretation entirely: the mood stabiliser is not incidental background, it is part of the tested intervention. A supplementary analysis found that lithium and valproate _levels_ did not correlate with ketamine's antidepressant efficacy — but that is a dose-response question within medicated patients, not evidence that unmedicated patients would fare the same. `[R-IV · D3 · P4 (pooled) · T2]` — **Suggestive.**

## D3. Hypomanic switch and activation risk

**Reported rates, single doses and short courses:** affective switch approximated **2.4%** in one review; **1.7% of 235 participants** across eight studies in another; **6 participants (2% of the pooled sample, 5 of whom received racemic ketamine)** developed hypomanic/manic symptoms in Fancy 2023. In Diazgranados 2010, **one participant in each arm** — ketamine and placebo — developed manic symptoms.

**The more important question is whether these trials could have detected switch at all, and mostly they could not.** The bipolar RCTs are single-dose or short-course crossover designs in samples of 15–18, with follow-up measured in days to a few weeks, in patients protected by a therapeutic mood stabiliser. **Absence of a switch signal in a 15-person, two-infusion, two-week trial is not evidence of safety.** A trial of that size has essentially no power to detect a 2–5% event rate.

**Repeated and maintenance dosing looks different, and should be read separately.** One maintenance series reported that **13 of 45 patients (28.9%) experienced symptoms consistent with a hypomanic or manic episode at some point during the maintenance phase**, with 16 events — approximately **one event per 2.7 patient-years**. That is an order of magnitude above the single-dose figures. It is an uncontrolled series, and in a bipolar population some hypomanic episodes would occur without any drug, so the attributable fraction is unknown. But the contrast between the single-dose and maintenance numbers is the honest summary: **short trials show almost nothing; longer exposure shows something.** `[R-IV/R-IN · D3–D4 · P4 (pooled) · T4]` — **Suggestive.**

## D4. Mixed features and activated presentation

**Entirely unstudied.** No trial of ketamine in bipolar depression with mixed features, in agitated depression, or in depression of uncertain polarity was identified, by any route, at any dose. No bipolar ketamine trial reported a mixed-features subgroup.

**The consequence is worth stating without hedging.** A person in daily anxiety and recurrent panic with a bipolar II diagnosis raises a genuine differential: anxious bipolar depression versus a depressive episode with mixed features. Those have different treatment implications, and the second is a state in which activating agents are conventionally approached with caution. **The presentation most likely to be read as "activated crisis" is simultaneously the one with the least evidence and the most theoretical concern about introducing a glutamatergic, activating agent.** Nothing in the literature tells us whether ketamine helps, does nothing, or destabilises in that state. `[PR1+PR2 · R-any · D-any · P5]` — **Unstudied.** What would test it: a trial stratifying bipolar depressed participants by DSM-5 mixed-features specifier with YMRS and activation endpoints.

**Direct answer: no. Across every pivotal SC and anxiety trial identified, a person with recent trauma, active panic attacks, acute stress disorder, or bipolar II presenting with anxiety was either explicitly excluded or simply not a population the trial recruited.**

# Context, durability, alternatives

## E1. Durability under ongoing stress

**Relapse is the rule, and it is fast.** After a **single** infusion, median time to relapse among responders is approximately **2 weeks**; after **six** infusions, approximately **6 weeks**. Repeated dosing produces cumulative benefit but does not confer durability: relapse after cessation occurs on average **18–19 days** post-infusion in one series, and in another all but one responder relapsed within a month.

**The SC-specific number is the most relevant one.** In KADS, cohort 2 remission was **19.6%** at the end of the 4-week course and **8.0%** four weeks after treatment stopped (midazolam 2.1%; OR 2.02, **P=0.4 — no longer statistically significant**). The authors' own conclusion: benefits "are not sustained for all remitters," and "ongoing treatment should be considered." In chronic PTSD, Feder 2021 found **median time to loss of response 27.5 days** among ketamine responders. `[R-SC and R-IV · D3–D4 · P3, P6 · T4]` — **Supported.**

**Ecological-fallacy caution.** "19.6% remission" is a group statistic. It does not mean any individual has a 19.6% chance — it means that in a heavily-screened treatment-resistant sample, about one in five reached a threshold score at one timepoint. It tells you nothing about how _this_ person, who does not resemble that sample, would respond, and it is not a personal prediction.

**The neuroplasticity-window question, asked directly.** The proposed mechanism — a transient window of enhanced synaptic plasticity (AMPAR activation, BDNF release, dendritic remodelling) — implies that what happens _during_ the window matters. **What the evidence says about closing that window onto an unresolved traumatic situation or ongoing instability: nothing directly.** No trial has stratified by ongoing stressor exposure, housing instability, financial or legal stress, or unresolved threat. `[PR5 · R-any · P-any · T4]` — **Unstudied.** The inference that a plasticity window closing onto a continuing stressor produces less durable benefit than one closing onto a stable environment is **mechanistically coherent and empirically untested**; I mark it as inference, **low-to-moderate confidence**. It is, however, the reasoning behind everything in §E2.

## E2. Ketamine plus psychotherapy in the post-traumatic window

**Pradhan et al. 2017 (TIMBER).** Randomised placebo-controlled trial, **n=20** PTSD (10 per arm): ketamine + TIMBER (Trauma Interventions using Mindfulness-Based Extinction and Reconsolidation) versus saline + TIMBER. Both groups received 12 TIMBER sessions (3 mini-sessions during infusion, day 2 and day 8; then 9 weekly 45-minute sessions). **Both groups improved on PCL and CAPS at 24 hours with no significant between-group difference.** But **duration of sustained response was longer with ketamine: 34.44 ± 19.12 days v. 16.50 ± 11.39 days.** `[TR4 · PR3 · R-IV · D3 · P6 · T4]` — **Suggestive** (n=20; the durability difference, not the acute response, is the finding).

**Shiroma et al.** Feasibility study, **n=12** veterans with chronic PTSD, 10 completers: **IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg 24 hours before** each weekly prolonged-exposure session over 3 weeks. Significant decrease in PTSD severity on PCL-5 and CGI-S at 4-month follow-up. `[TR4 · PR3 · R-IV · D3 · P6 · T4]` — **Suggestive** (open-label feasibility, n=12, no control).

**The tension with §A1, stated directly.** If timing near a trauma is mechanistically risky because NMDA antagonism can interfere with consolidation and extinction, does adding structured therapy mitigate or compound that? **The literature does not answer this.** Both readings are available: therapy during the window could channel the plasticity toward corrective learning (mitigation), or a therapy session that deliberately retrieves the trauma memory _during_ a period of NMDA blockade sits exactly where the reconsolidation concern is sharpest (compounding). Neither TIMBER nor the Shiroma protocol was conducted in the early window — both enrolled **chronic** PTSD. Note that the two protocols differ in a way that may matter: TIMBER delivered mini-sessions **during** the infusion; Shiroma delivered exposure **24 hours after**. Nobody has compared them head to head. `[TR1–TR3 · PR3 · R-any · P6]` — **Unstudied.**

**Benzodiazepines after trauma, in detail, because this is the realistic alternative.** Guina et al. 2015 (_J Psychiatr Pract_): systematic review and meta-analysis, **18 clinical trials and observational studies, 5,236 participants**. Findings: benzodiazepines were associated with **no improvement in PTSD-related outcomes**; and **use in patients with recent trauma significantly increased the risk of developing PTSD**. Beyond general adverse effects, benzodiazepine use in PTSD patients was associated with **worse overall severity, worse psychotherapy outcomes, aggression, depression and substance use**. The proposed mechanism is that benzodiazepines interfere with the learning that recovery depends on — numbing emotion, reducing learning efficiency, and impairing memory consolidation of material learned in therapy. The authors concluded that risks tend to outweigh short-term benefits. `[TR1–TR3 · PR1–PR3 · P6 · T4]` — **Supported** for harm after recent trauma; note that at least one more recent population-wide analysis following a mass-trauma exposure has reported findings not showing increased PTSD risk, so this is not entirely uncontested — but Guina remains the larger and more directly on-point synthesis.

**The important structural point:** benzodiazepines and ketamine share a mechanistic concern in this window — both are agents that act on memory and learning processes at exactly the time those processes are doing consequential work. The benzodiazepine literature is the one place where that concern has actually been tested at scale, and it came back negative. That is not proof that ketamine behaves the same way — different mechanism, different pharmacology — but it is the closest empirical analogue available, and it is not reassuring.

## E4. Unsupervised at-home routes (R-SL) under instability

Brief and unambiguous.

**Regulatory status, dated 2026-08-30.** On **31 December 2025** the DEA and HHS published the **Fourth Temporary Extension of COVID-19 Telemedicine Flexibilities for Prescription of Controlled Medications**, permitting DEA-registered practitioners to continue prescribing Schedule II–V controlled substances via telehealth without a prior in-person evaluation, **through 31 December 2026**. A proposed permanent rule ("Special Registrations for Telemedicine and Limited State Telemedicine Registrations") was published in **January 2025** and has **not been finalised**. State medical boards and corporate-practice-of-medicine doctrines govern separately. **This is a moving target; anything read about it more than a few months old is likely stale.**

**FDA position.** On **10 October 2023** the FDA issued a public warning on compounded ketamine products, stating it is **"not aware of evidence to suggest that it is safer, is more effective, or works faster than medications that are FDA-approved"** for psychiatric disorders, and specifically flagging that **at-home administration is especially risky because of the lack of monitoring**, placing patients at risk for serious adverse events, misuse and abuse. Named concerns: sedation, dissociation, changes in vital signs, abuse and misuse, psychiatric events, blood-pressure increases, slowed breathing, and lower urinary tract and bladder symptoms.

**Why this is worse for this specific reader.** Every SC and IV protocol reviewed here retained a **2-hour supervised observation window**; two of the three ketamine-attributed serious adverse events in the phase 3 SC trial were acute psychological events (a major dissociative episode; an auditory hallucination) that occurred in a monitored setting. The acute-effect profile in §A4 — chest heaviness, dizziness, confusion, derealisation — is the somatic content of panic. Unsupervised dosing removes the two things that manage that: a clinician present, and a structured setting. Under psychosocial instability, self-directed dose escalation and dependence risk are additionally live. **The conclusion is not in doubt: unsupervised at-home ketamine during an acute post-traumatic crisis is not supported by anything in this literature.**

# Red flags

- **🚩 The trials never enrolled this person.** Recent trauma, active panic, acute stress disorder, and BD-II-with-anxiety were excluded or simply not recruited across every pivotal SC and anxiety trial. Any recommendation here is an extrapolation across timing, diagnosis and chronicity simultaneously.
- **🚩 The peri-traumatic timing question is genuinely unresolved, and the harm signals sit in the early window.** Schönenberg found increased dissociation, re-experiencing, hyperarousal and avoidance at 3 days post-event in ketamine recipients. Mion, the largest and only multivariable-adjusted study, found nothing. Nobody has looked at 1–3 months.
- **🚩 The acute session in a panicking person.** Three in four SC recipients at escalated doses experienced dissociation; one in three experienced chest heaviness. That is panic phenomenology delivered by injection. Two of three ketamine-attributed serious adverse events in the phase 3 SC trial were acute psychological events, and participants discontinued for **increased anxiety**.
- **🚩 The polarity / mixed-features question is the emptiest cell in the entire literature.** If the presentation is a mixed state rather than anxious depression, there is no evidence at all, and the theoretical concern about an activating agent is at its highest.
- **🚩 The bipolar evidence is not BD-II evidence, and it is not unmedicated-patient evidence.** Every positive result was generated in pooled BD-I/BD-II samples maintained on therapeutic lithium or valproate. There is no SC bipolar trial.
- **🚩 The dose that worked is the dose that dissociated.** In the only adequately-powered SC trial, fixed 0.5 mg/kg failed and escalation to 0.9 mg/kg worked — with dissociation rising from 48.5% to 77.4%. Choosing SC does not reliably buy benefit without the session.
- **🚩 Durability collapses fast.** SC remission fell from 19.6% to 8.0% four weeks after stopping. Anything that works here will raise a maintenance question immediately.
- **🚩 Undisclosed-interest risk is real in this literature.** The SC anxiety programme's investigators hold a formulation-development contract with a pharmaceutical company and a ketamine tablet patent. The chronic-PTSD investigators' institution holds a ketamine use patent. Multiple KADS authors have industry relationships, including equity in ketamine-adjacent companies. None of this is hidden — all of it is disclosed — but it means near-total absence of independent replication.
- **🚩 Unsupervised at-home dosing during instability.** Not supported by anything in this literature; explicitly warned against by the FDA (10 Oct 2023); removes the 2-hour supervised observation window that every published protocol retained.

# What would change this answer

- **A controlled trial dosing in TR2–TR3 with psychiatric endpoints.** This is the single largest gap. Until it exists, the timing question stays open.
- **A panic-disorder trial.** Any randomised comparator-controlled trial with panic-attack frequency as the primary outcome would convert the central `Unstudied` into evidence.
- **A parallel-group SC dose-ranging trial** randomising two or three SC doses against a common comparator, rather than escalating within participants — which would give a real dose-response curve instead of one confounded with cumulative exposure.
- **A PK/PD model that includes dissociation.** Adding CADSS to Abuhelwa's MADRS/BP/HR framework across SC and IV administration would directly test the hypothesis in the primary question.
- **A mediation analysis of dissociation against anxiolytic outcome**, which has never been done.
- **A BD-II subgroup breakdown** from the existing pooled bipolar trials, or a BD-II-only trial; and any bipolar trial in unmedicated patients or with a mixed-features stratification.
- **Independent replication of the SC anxiety programme** by a group without a formulation-development interest.

**What a prescriber needs to know about the individual that this report cannot supply:** the exact interval since the trauma and whether the acute stress picture is resolving or entrenching; whether the presentation meets criteria for panic disorder, acute stress disorder, early PTSD, anxious bipolar depression, or a mixed state — and how confident that determination is; the full medication list, especially whether a mood stabiliser is on board at therapeutic level; substance use and any prior dissociative or panic response to medication or anaesthesia; cardiovascular status and baseline blood pressure; baseline urinary symptoms and liver function; what psychotherapy is available and when it could start; whether the traumatic situation is over or ongoing; and what the person's own priorities are between speed of relief and avoiding an aversive session.

# Questions for a prescriber

**On SC dose selection**

1. The phase 3 SC trial failed at a fixed 0.5 mg/kg and succeeded only after escalating to 0.75–0.9 mg/kg — but that was in treatment-resistant depression, not anxiety. What dose would you start at for an anxiety-predominant presentation, and what is that number based on?
2. The SC anxiety data come from one group of 12 patients where 0.25 mg/kg produced only minor changes. Would you start below the dose that separated, accepting a likely non-response, in order to test tolerability first?
3. SC bioavailability was estimated at about 64% in the one study that modelled SC, IM and IV together — not the ~90% often quoted. Does that change the dose you would choose relative to an IV protocol?
4. If a first session is intolerable, is the plan to reduce the dose, stop, or switch route — and how is that decided in advance rather than in the moment?

**On timing relative to the trauma**

5. Given that no controlled trial has ever dosed ketamine in the first three months after a trauma for psychiatric benefit, what is your reasoning for treating now rather than after the acute phase — and what would make you want to wait?
6. The concern about NMDA antagonism during memory consolidation is strongest in the first days and attenuates over time. How much weight do you give it at this specific interval, and is there anything about the presentation that raises or lowers it?
7. Schönenberg found worsened dissociation, re-experiencing and hyperarousal at 3 days in accident victims given ketamine; Mion, in a larger adjusted analysis, found nothing. How do you weigh those against each other?

**On bipolar II and polarity screening**

8. Every positive bipolar ketamine result was generated in patients maintained on therapeutic lithium or valproate, and the trials pooled bipolar I with bipolar II without publishing a subtype breakdown. If a mood stabiliser is not on board at therapeutic level, what does that evidence actually tell us?
9. How confident are you that this presentation is anxious depression rather than a depressive episode with mixed features — and what would you look for to distinguish them? Ketamine has never been studied in mixed features at all.
10. Single-dose bipolar trials of 15–18 people report switch rates around 2%, but one maintenance series reported hypomanic or manic symptoms in 29% of patients at some point. If this becomes maintenance treatment, how will activation be monitored, and by whom?

**On the session and on dissociation**

11. Roughly three in four people at escalated SC doses experience dissociation and light-headedness, and about one in three chest heaviness. For someone whose daily problem is panic, what is the plan if the session itself triggers a panic attack?
12. Is dissociation something you are aiming for, tolerating, or trying to minimise — and what is that position based on? (The evidence does not establish that dissociation is necessary for benefit, and the anxiolytic version of the question has never been studied.)
13. Dissociation scores fell from 20 to 8.8 over 14 weeks in the maintenance anxiety series. Is a difficult first session a reason to stop or a reason to expect the second to be easier?

**On durability, monitoring and alternatives**

14. SC remission fell from about 20% to 8% four weeks after treatment stopped. What is the maintenance plan, what is the exit plan, and at what point do we conclude it is not working?
15. Every published SC protocol used a 2-hour supervised observation period after each injection. What monitoring will actually be in place, and where?
16. Benzodiazepines are the most likely alternative for daily panic, and the largest synthesis found they increase PTSD risk when given after recent trauma. Given that, what is the comparison you are actually making — ketamine against trauma-focused therapy, against an SSRI, against watchful waiting, or against a benzodiazepine?
17. Early trauma-focused CBT is the best-evidenced option in this window and guidance suggests starting 2–3 weeks post-exposure. Is that available, and if it is, does ketamine come before it, alongside it, or only if it fails?

# Methods note

## Phase 0 pre-commitment, reproduced

Written before evidence gathering:

**If the answer to the primary question were YES, I expected to find:** (a) at least one controlled trial administering ketamine in TR1–TR3 with a psychiatric endpoint showing benefit; (b) SC dose-ranging evidence in an anxiety-disorder population with an active comparator, separating at doses below the strongly dissociative range; (c) peri-traumatic cohort data leaning consistently protective; (d) evidence that dissociation is separable from benefit, so the aversive session could be minimised; (e) some enrolment of acutely-presenting, recently-traumatised, or panic-disorder patients in the pivotal trials.

**If NO, I expected to find:** (a) no deliberate early-window psychiatric trials at all; (b) SC evidence confined to treatment-resistant depression and chronic refractory anxiety; (c) peri-traumatic data conflicting or leaning toward harm; (d) systematic exclusion of this presentation from the pivotal trials; (e) durability that collapses on cessation.

**Distinguishing evidence:** the existence or non-existence of a TR1–TR3 psychiatric trial; the eligibility criteria of the pivotal SC and anxiety trials; whether SC dose-response curves for benefit and for dissociation separate or run parallel.

**Result:** the NO-shaped pattern is what I found on (a) through (e). One partial exception runs the other way: the population PK/PD modelling (Abuhelwa 2022) shows a large formal separation between the therapeutic and haemodynamic concentration-response curves, which weakly favours the decoupling hypothesis in the primary question — though it did not model dissociation.

## Phase 6 verification

Approximately **64 quantitative claims and citations** were carried toward this report and independently re-checked against primary or near-primary sources for identifier resolution, claim-to-source fit, exact figures, route match, population match, timing match, and exclusion compliance.

- **Revised: 9.**
  1. SC bioavailability — initial secondary sources gave ~90%; corrected to **~64%** from the primary population PK study (Abuhelwa 2022). The ~90% figure could not be traced to a psychiatric-dose human PK study, and a 2023 route-bioavailability review omits SC entirely.
  2. **McGhee 2008 and McGhee 2014 were being conflated** in secondary summaries as one study; separated into a protective finding and its non-replication.
  3. **Taylor 2018** was returning as a uniformly positive trial; the **VAS-Anxiety outcome was null (P=0.95)** and is now reported alongside the positive LSAS result.
  4. **Diazgranados 2010** n reported variously as 18 (randomised) and 17 (analysed); both are now given.
  5. **Feder 2021** CADSS figures, full eligibility criteria and COI statement could not be obtained at full text; downgraded to abstract-level reporting with the limitation stated inline rather than backfilled from Feder 2014.
  6. **The SC-later-Tmax premise** in the primary question was carried in as given; **no human psychiatric-dose SC Tmax was located**, so it was downgraded from premise to untested inference. This is the most consequential revision in the report.
  7. **Prazosin** was initially represented only by positive small trials; the larger negative Raskind 2018 _NEJM_ trial (n=304) was added and the label changed to Contested.
  8. **KADS cohort 1** was initially summarised only as "not different"; the full non-separating figures (remission, response and MADRS change, all null) are now reported by name.
  9. **Lanicemine** was initially framed as a clean negative for the dissociation hypothesis; reframed as ambiguous, since a failed programme confounds "no dissociation" with "weaker drug" and a 39% placebo response.

- **Deleted: 3.**
  1. **Grunebaum et al. 2017** (bipolar, n=16) removed as efficacy evidence — enrolled for suicidal ideation. Retained by name in §A5 as an exclusion disclosure, with its negative depression result noted there.
  2. The esketamine acute-suicidality registration programme (ASPIRE, SUI3001/3002) removed as efficacy evidence; label safety data retained in harms only.
  3. Practitioner/clinic marketing sources encountered during searching were not cited for any efficacy or dose claim.

- **Sections left thin rather than backfilled:** panic disorder (§A2), symptom-cluster effects in PTSD (§A3), anxiolytic dissociation mediation (§C3), mixed features (§D4), plasticity-window closure under ongoing stress (§E1), and combined therapy in the early window (§E2) are all reported as **Unstudied** rather than supported by weaker adjacent sources.

- **Full-text access limits, disclosed:** Glue 2020, Tully 2022, Mion 2017, the IJNP bipolar systematic review, Abuhelwa 2022 and Feder 2021 were verified at **abstract or structured-summary level**; publisher access blocks prevented full-text reading. Their figures should be confirmed at source before being relied on clinically.

## Drift check

**Did any conclusion move toward what I expected?** Yes, once, and it was corrected in the opposite direction from convenience. I came in expecting to confirm that SC produces a later, lower peak than IV — the premise of the primary question and an attractive one, since it would have made the Q3 answer practically useful. I could not find that measurement in humans at psychiatric doses. Rather than presenting the model-implied inference as a finding, I downgraded it to `Unstudied` and stated what would test it. The rest of the conclusions moved _away_ from what a reader hoping for a green light would want: the SC clinical data show benefit and dissociation rising together, and the phase 3 trial's failed arm was the low-dose one.

## How each failure mode was handled

1. **Motivated synthesis** — the unwelcome answers are stated first: the trials never enrolled this person; the fixed low SC dose failed; durability collapses; the mixed-features cell is empty.
2. **Smuggled suicidality evidence** — §A5 names what was removed, including the one bipolar trial dropped for it; suicidality appears only under harms.
3. **Route laundering** — every dose claim is marked SC-native or `[IV-derived]` in the dose table and inline; §B1 opens with the bioavailability correction.
4. **Timing laundering** — Feder 2014's mean 11.9–14.2 years since trauma is reported at the point of use, and §A3 states explicitly what the chronic base does not establish.
5. **Pooled-bipolar laundering** — the pooling rule is applied in the D1 table row by row; the section's ceiling label is stated as **Suggestive** and no BD-II-specific claim is made.
6. **Dissociation-as-mechanism overreach** — §C4 gives an explicit verdict with a label and states that "dissociation is irrelevant" is also stronger than the evidence.
7. **Positive-result bias** — named negatives: KADS cohort 1 (fixed 0.5 mg/kg), George 2017 at 0.1 mg/kg, Glue at 0.25 mg/kg, Taylor 2018 VAS-Anxiety, lanicemine phase IIb, Lii 2023, Raskind 2018, Pradhan's null 24-hour comparison, KADS 4-week follow-up.
8. **Undisclosed commercial interest** — Douglas Pharmaceuticals contract and tablet patent (Glue programme), Mount Sinai ketamine use patent (Feder programme), and KADS author disclosures including equity in ketamine-adjacent companies are all noted at point of use.
9. **Both-sidesing via "Contested"** — each Contested label names the stronger design: Mion for peri-traumatic timing; the replication-failure side for dissociation-as-mediator; Raskind for prazosin.
10. **Ecological fallacy** — addressed explicitly in §E1 for the KADS remission figure.
11. **Green-lighting** — the report resolves into an evidence map and a question list, not a recommendation. Every path forward routes through a prescriber, and §E4 is unambiguous about unsupervised dosing.

# Gaps and limitations

- **The central question is unanswerable from the trial literature**, not because of a search failure but because the trials excluded the presentation. That is the finding, and it should not be read as a soft "more research needed."
- **The SC evidence base is very small.** Setting aside KADS (n=174), the entire SC literature comprises studies of 12, 15, 16, 20 and 24 participants, several drawing on overlapping samples, and two research groups produced nearly all of it.
- **No SC trial exists in PTSD, in bipolar depression, in panic disorder, or in acute stress disorder.** Every claim about SC in this report comes from treatment-resistant depression or chronic refractory GAD/SAD.
- **No SC-versus-IV head-to-head at matched dose** with PK, dissociation and efficacy measured together. This is the study that would settle Q2 and Q3 simultaneously.
- **Anxiolytic and antidepressant dose-response curves cannot be compared** — different populations, groups, designs.
- **Publisher access blocked full-text verification** for six sources (listed above); those are reported at abstract level and flagged.
- **Registry searching was not exhaustive.** I searched for early-window psychiatric trials and found none, but a systematic registry sweep across ClinicalTrials.gov, ANZCTR, EU-CTR and ISRCTN was outside what this pass could complete. The claim "no such trial exists" should be read as "none was found by targeted search."
- **Regulatory and cost statements are dated 2026-08-30 and move quickly** — particularly the DEA telemedicine flexibilities, which currently expire 31 December 2026.
- **This review does not assess individual suitability, drug interactions, or cardiovascular fitness for treatment.** Those are prescriber questions and cannot be answered from literature.
